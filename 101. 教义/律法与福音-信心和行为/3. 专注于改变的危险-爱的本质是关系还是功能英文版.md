# When Change Becomes the Goal: Are We Still Loving the Person?

*The danger of focusing on change: is love relational or functional?*

*This essay grew out of my own questions and struggles, and most of its content came out of conversations with AI. If anything here is off, I welcome the discussion. (First published in Chinese on Substack, November 9, 2025; this is a revised version.)*

## 1. A Common Unease

In Christian circles we often hear teaching like this:

“Real change doesn’t come from moral effort but from the renewal of the Holy Spirit.” “Don’t rely on self-discipline; rely on grace.” “The power of the gospel can change people.”

These words seem beyond reproach. Yet many people, when they hear them, feel an unease deep down that they can’t quite name: something seems off, but it is hard to say what.

This unease is often clearer in real life:

- The pastor stresses that “grace can change you,” but you always feel an invisible pressure: “Have I changed? Have I changed enough?”
- Parents say, “I love you, and God loves you,” followed immediately by “so you need to…”
- The counselor says, “Let the Holy Spirit renew you,” but the focus always seems to be on “Have you gotten better?”

The core question of this essay is:

**Have we, without realizing it, made “changing people” the goal, and turned “love / grace / the Holy Spirit” into the means of reaching that goal?**

Is there anything wrong with making change the goal? Isn’t that change for the person’s own good? If I love someone and so hope they will get better, what’s wrong with that?

My question is: **when love becomes a tool, is it still love?**

## 2. Two Very Different Frameworks

### Framework A: Functional / Outcome-Oriented

**Basic logic:**

```
Diagnosis: People have problems and need to get better
Method comparison: moral self-discipline (ineffective) vs. renewal by the Spirit (effective)
Goal: People are changed
Verification: Observe whether change has happened
```

**Typical phrases:**

- “The law can’t change people, but the Spirit can”
- “We need the renewal of the Spirit so that we can become holy”
- “Real change comes from grace”

**Underlying structure:**

```
God’s love / the Spirit (means) → changes the heart (process) →
person gets better (goal) → God is glorified (ultimate purpose)
```

**Core questions:** “How do we make people better?” “Which method works better?” “Have I changed?”

### Framework B: Relational / Being-Oriented

**Basic logic:**

```
Truth: God loves sinners (he loves this person, for their own sake)
Grace: makes sinners into children (a change of identity)
Nature: children want to be like their father (the essence of the relationship)
Calling: live out your true identity
Result: change happens naturally (a by-product of love)
```

**Typical phrases:**

- “God loves you not because you will get better, but because you are his child”
- “You already are a new creation; live out your true identity”
- “Change is not the condition of love but the fruit of love”

**Underlying structure:**

```
God loves people (an end in itself) → adopts them as children (a gift of being) →
children want to respond (a natural inclination) → a sanctified life (the overflow of relationship)
```

**Core questions:** “How does God love me?” “Who am I?” “How do I respond to this love?”

## 3. The Subtle Betrayal of Language

The difference between these two frameworks often hides in very subtle differences of wording.

### Contrast 1: Purpose vs. Essence

| Functional framework | Relational framework |
|---|---|
| God loves you in order to change you | God loves you, period |
| The Spirit renews you so that you can become holy | The Spirit makes you a child of God |
| Grace can remake you | Grace has already made you God’s own |
| The gospel produces new life | The gospel is new life |
| **Through** grace, you can become holy | **In** grace, you are God’s child |
| Because you are loved, **you must**… | Because you are loved, **you want to**… |

It looks like a difference of a word or two, but behind it lie two completely different theological worlds.

### Contrast 2: Transaction vs. Covenant

**Marks of instrumental language:**

- “God gave you X, so you must do Y” → implies: Y is the payback for X
- “Because of grace, you should…” → implies: grace creates an obligation
- “The renewal of the Spirit is able to…” → implies: the measure is “power”

**Marks of relational language:**

- “God loves you as a person (full stop)” → establishes first: there is no transaction in love
- “Because you are God’s child, you want to…” → emphasizes: desire flows from identity, not obligation
- “The Spirit makes you…” → focuses on: a change in being, not just in function

### Contrast 3: Condition vs. Assurance

Listen to the emotional tone of these statements:

**Functional:**

“Only when you let the Spirit renew you can you change” → what the hearer feels: Am I doing enough?

**Relational:**

“The Spirit has already made you a new creation; you already want to please God” → what the hearer feels: I have already been changed!

## 4. What Does Scripture Say?

### The Nature of God’s Love: Unconditional Giving

**Romans 5:8**

> “But God demonstrates his own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us.”

The order in time reveals the nature of love:

- ❌ Not: we have a repentant heart → God loves us
- ✓ But: we are still sinners → God already loves us

“While we were still sinners”: even before grace could change us.

**1 John 4:10**

> “This is love: not that we loved God, but that he loved us and sent his Son as an atoning sacrifice for our sins.”

The definition of love:

- Not attraction (because the other is lovable)
- Not exchange (expecting a return)
- But: active, sacrificial giving

**John 17:24**

> “Father, I want those you have given me to be with me where I am”

**Jesus’ prayer:** that they may be with me.

Not “make them perfect,” but “**with me**.”

Being together is itself the goal.

### But Love Really Does Bring Change

**1 John 4:19**

> “We love because he first loved us.”

The chain of logic:

```
God’s love (A) → we are loved (B) → we respond with love (C)
```

The key: C is not the purpose of A; C is the natural overflow of B.

**Ephesians 2:8–10**

> “For it is by grace you have been saved, through faith… For we are God’s handiwork, created in Christ Jesus to do good works.”

The order:

1. Saved by grace (loved / accepted)
2. Made God’s handiwork (a new identity)
3. To do good works (result / calling)

“To do good works” is not a purpose but a design:

- ❌ Not: God saved you in order to get you to do good works (a transaction)
- ✓ But: God made you a new person who does good works (a nature)

### The Identity of Children: Where Grace Arrives

**Galatians 4:4–5**

> “But when the set time had fully come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, to redeem those under the law, that we might receive adoption to sonship.”

The purpose of redemption: not (first of all) to change our behavior, but to **restore our identity as children**.

**Galatians 4:6–7**

> “Because you are his sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, the Spirit who calls out, ‘Abba, Father.’ So you are no longer a slave, but God’s child.”

| The heart of a slave | The heart of a child |
|---|---|
| Fear | Intimacy (“Abba”) |
| Earns wages by performance | Enjoys an inheritance by identity |
| Focus: Am I doing enough? | Focus: I am the Father’s child! |

The identity of a child is itself the summit of grace. It is not an intermediate step toward some other goal, nor a tool for reaching the goal of change.

**Romans 8:15–17**

> “The Spirit you received does not make you slaves, so that you live in fear again; rather, the Spirit you received brought about your adoption to sonship.”

A slave earns wages by performance; a child enjoys an inheritance by identity.

## 5. What the Two Frameworks Do in Practice

### In Personal Life

**The experience cycle of the functional framework:**

```
I’m saved → now I need to “let the Spirit change me” → self-examination: Have I changed?
         ↓                                                  ↓
  I don’t see enough change                          I see some change
         ↓                                                  ↓
     anxiety / doubt                                 pride / comparison
         ↓                                                  ↓
                      a cycle that is never satisfied
```

**Inner dialogue:**

“Is God’s grace really enough? Why am I still the same…”

“Maybe I need more prayer / Bible reading / obedience”

**Clinical symptoms:** spiritual perfectionism, hidden performance, anxiety loops, spiritual burnout

**The experience cycle of the relational framework:**

```
I am loved by God → I am his child → I want to be like my Father → I live it out day by day
         ↓                                                  ↓
      I fail                                             I grow
         ↓                                                  ↓
still his child, back to the Father’s arms       thankful for grace, keep going
         ↓                                                  ↓
                        continuing to grow in love
```

**Inner dialogue:**

“I am God’s child; this is certain”

“I failed today, but my Father still loves me, and I go back to him”

**Clinical signs:** real freedom, lasting motivation, genuine joy, sustainability

### In Raising Children: A Scenario

**Scenario: a child fails an exam**

**Functional response:**

“We love you so much and have given so much for you, all because we want you to study well. How could you do this badly? You need to let God change your attitude toward studying.”

**Message conveyed:** our love is an investment and expects a return; your worth = your performance; you have let us down

(This is in fact the inevitable result of a functional understanding of “love.”)

**Possible reactions:** guilt, outward compliance with inner distance; or rebellion: “I’ll never meet your expectations”

**Relational response:**

“I love you no matter what score you get. You are my child, and that will never change. (A hug.)

But I care about you, and I want to help you find where the difficulty is. Because I know your ability to learn is a gift from God, I want you to be able to enjoy learning, not be crushed by it.

Let’s face this together, okay?”

**Message conveyed:** love is unconditional; I care about your struggle itself; an invitation to talk, not an imposed demand

(Support, companionship, facing it together.)

**Possible reactions:** feeling understood and accepted; more willing to open up about real struggles; perhaps rethinking things

## 6. The Philosophical Roots of the Problem

The eighteenth-century philosopher Immanuel Kant set down one of the cornerstones of moral philosophy:

> “Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means, but always at the same time as an end.”

### Applied to Faith

**Treating people as means:**

- I love this person → in order to make them into a certain kind of person
- I raise my child → in order for them to succeed / be holy
- God gives grace → in order to turn sinners into saints

**Treating people as ends:**

- I love this person → I simply love this person
- I raise my child → because they are my child
- God gives grace → because he loves you and wants you with him

### An Existentialist Insight

Existentialist thought, especially Martin Buber, distinguishes two kinds of relationship:

**I–It:**

- I treat the other as an object
- The other’s value lies in function / usefulness
- Example: I “love” this hammer because it drives nails

**I–Thou:**

- I treat the other as a subject
- The other’s value lies in who they are
- Example: I love my spouse; I simply love this person

**A dangerous confusion:**

When we say “the Spirit can change people,” if the focus is on “change” (the result), we quietly demote the person from subject to object.

## 7. Deeper Theological Distinctions

### Where Change Belongs

The key question: **where does change belong in the logic of love?**

❌ **The wrong places (functional):**

Model 1: change as the condition of love

```
You changed → so God / I love you
```

(Obviously wrong)

Model 2: change as the purpose of love

```
God loves you → in order to change you
```

(A more hidden error)

Model 3: change as the proof of love

```
God loves you → so you will change → your change proves God’s love
```

(Dangerous)

✓ **The right place (relational):**

```
God loves you (A: the cause)
    ↓
makes you his child (B: a gift of being)
    ↓
the child wants to be like the Father (C: a new nature)
    ↓
growing in grace (D: the expression of life)
    ↓
change happens (E: the natural result)
```

Understanding this chain:

- E is not the purpose of A
- E is the result of D
- D is the expression of C
- C is the essence of B
- B is the gift of A

In terms of a tree:

- ❌ Not: bearing fruit in order to prove it is a good tree
- ✓ But: a good tree naturally bears fruit

### Being Comes Before Doing

A Latin theological maxim: **Agere sequitur esse** (action follows being).

The core principle: what you do flows from what you are; not the other way around, where what you do determines what you are.

**Applied to the doctrine of salvation:**

| Step | Functional error | Relational truth |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Repentance (you do it) | God’s love (God does it) |
| 2 | Change (you do it) | New birth (God does it) |
| 3 | Therefore saved | Identity as a child (a gift) |
| 4 | Keep changing (maintenance) | Therefore wanting to obey (overflow) |

**John 1:12–13**

> “Yet to all who did receive him, to those who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God—children… born of God.”

The order:

1. Born of God (God’s action)
2. Becoming children (a change of being)
3. Believing in his name (response)
4. Receiving him (action)

### How Justification, Adoption, and Sanctification Relate

These are not stages in time but different aspects of one reality, union with Christ:

```
              Union with Christ
            (a once-for-all fact)
                     |
        _____________________________
        |             |             |
  Justification    Adoption     New birth
   (legal         (family       (new nature)
    standing)      identity)
        |             |             |
        ——————————————|——————————————
                      |
               Sanctification
           (living out the new nature)
```

The moment you come to faith:

- You are justified (legally, not guilty)
- You are adopted (relationally, a child)
- You are born again (by nature, a new creation)
- Sanctification begins (growth in experience)

This means: sanctification is not a “phase-two project” after justification, but the gradual realization in life of your identity as a child.

**Romans 6:11**

> “In the same way, count yourselves dead to sin but alive to God in Christ Jesus.”

Not “will be” (future), but “**are**” (present).

## 8. The Paradox of Letting Go

### When You Stop Making Change the Goal, Change Happens

**The vine and the branches (John 15:4–5)**

> “Remain in me, as I also remain in you. No branch can bear fruit by itself; it must remain in the vine… apart from me you can do nothing.”

In the Greek of just these few verses (15:4–10), the word “remain” occurs ten times!

**A shift of focus:**

- ❌ Not: “How do I bear fruit?”
- ✓ But: “**Remain in the vine**”

**The branch does not need to:**

- ❌ Strive to “manufacture” fruit
- ❌ Worry, “Have I borne fruit?”
- ❌ Compare, “Do I have more fruit than the other branches?”

**The branch only needs to:** ✓ stay on the vine → and the fruit comes naturally

**Spiritual application:**

Your responsibility: “remain in Christ” (keep the union, the relationship of being)

God’s responsibility: “bear fruit” (produce the change)

Your focus should be: Am I enjoying my relationship with Christ, or running from it?

Not: How much fruit have I borne?

**The paradox:**

When you stop staring at the fruit and simply concentrate on remaining in Christ, the fruit is more abundant.

### The Witness of the Saints

**Augustine’s turning point:**

**Early on:** trying to overcome sin by willpower → failure, struggle

**The turning point:** reading Romans 13:14, “clothe yourselves with the Lord Jesus Christ”

**The insight:** not “try to become like him,” but “put him on” (clothe yourself with him, be united with him)

**The result:** later, in his *Confessions*, he prayed:

> “You command continence… grant what you command, and command what you will.” (*Confessions*, Book X)

He changed because he first received grace.

## 9. Applied to Preaching: A Comparison

**Text: Matthew 18:21–35 (the unforgiving servant)**

### A Functional Sermon Outline

```
1. Law: the sin of unforgiveness (condemnation)
2. Gospel: God’s forgiveness (comfort)
3. Command: so you must forgive
4. Power: by the Spirit, you can do it
```

What the congregation thinks as they leave: “I need to try hard to forgive” (focus: my performance)

### A Relational Sermon Outline

**1. Recognize what we have received**

- The master cancelled an astronomical debt of “ten thousand bags of gold”
- You could never repay it, but God says, “I have cancelled it!”

**2. Recognize who we are**

- You are no longer a debtor; you are a child
- You are the child of a generous Father

**3. Recognize our struggle**

- The servant seized a fellow servant who owed him “a hundred silver coins”
- Compare: ten thousand bags of gold vs. a hundred silver coins
- We too often forget how much we have been forgiven

**4. Live out the identity of a child**

- When someone hurts you: remember you have been forgiven ten thousand bags of gold
- Forgive, not because “you should” (obligation)
- but because you already are a forgiven person (the overflow of identity)

**5. Growing in grace**

- Admit it: forgiving is hard
- The promise: when you are weak, the Father still loves you
- The hope: each time you choose to forgive, you taste the joy of freedom

What the congregation thinks as they leave: “I have been loved so generously; I want to share this love” (focus: the Father’s love and my identity)

## 10. A Different Story from Beginning to End

### Creation: For Fellowship

❌ Not:

```
God created people → to get glory / service
```

✓ But:

```
The triune God, complete in the fellowship of love,
was willing to share that love and fellowship,
and so created human beings in his image,
so that they might enjoy God and be with God.
```

**Genesis 1:27, 31**

God created human beings in his own image, then looked at all that he had made and said it was “**very good**.”

Not “useful,” but “good.”

### The Fall: A Broken Relationship

**Genesis 3:8–10**

> “They hid from the LORD God among the trees of the garden.”

**The tragedy is not only that** human beings broke a command (a failure of function).

**The tragedy is even more that** they hid from God’s face (a broken relationship).

**The essence of sin:** not only “doing wrong,” but “**leaving the relationship**.”

### Redemption: The Father Seeks His Children

**Luke 15: the parable of the prodigal son**

When the younger son comes back, he says (rehearsed):

> “I am no longer worthy to be called your son; make me like one of your hired servants.”

The father’s response:

- ✓ He cuts him off (does not let him finish the line about being a hired servant)
- ✓ He orders: “Bring the best robe… Put a ring on his finger”
- ✓ He declares: “For this son of mine was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found”

**What the father refuses:** a functional relationship (hired servant = earning wages by performance)

**What the father insists on:** the identity of a son (a son no matter what)

The father does not care whether his son can “work well.” The father cares only that his son has come home.

**This is the gospel:** not God assessing whether you can change, but God running toward you to restore the relationship.

**Luke 19:10**

> “For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost.”

- ❌ Not: waiting until sinners have repented to a certain degree, then accepting them
- ✓ But: actively seeking those still in their sin

### Sanctification: Children Growing Up

❌ Not: working hard to meet the Father’s standard so that you can stay in the house

✓ But: growing up in the Father’s house, becoming more and more like the Father

**Ephesians 4:13–16**

> “…until we all reach unity in the faith and in the knowledge of the Son of God and become mature, attaining to the whole measure of the fullness of Christ… [as the body] builds itself up in love.”

**The goal of growth:** knowing the Son of God (a deepening relationship)

**The setting of growth:** in love (not in fear, demand, or comparison)

**Hebrews 12:6, 10**

> “The Lord disciplines the one he loves… God disciplines us for our good.”

**The motive of discipline:** love

**The purpose of discipline:** our good (not meeting a standard)

**The premise of discipline:** you are already a child

### Glory: Complete Presence

**Revelation 21:3**

> “Look! God’s dwelling place is now among the people, and he will dwell with them… God himself will be with them.”

The heart of the new heavens and new earth:

- ❌ Not: “The remodeling is finally finished”
- ✓ But: “**complete, eternal fellowship**”

**Revelation 22:4**

> “They will see his face.”

The ultimate hope:

- ❌ Not: “I am finally perfect”
- ✓ But: “**I will see his face and be with him forever**”

Being with him is itself heaven.

## 11. Answering Common Misunderstandings

### Misunderstanding 1: “Isn’t this cheap grace?”

In *The Cost of Discipleship*, Dietrich Bonhoeffer described cheap grace as “the preaching of forgiveness without requiring repentance… grace without discipleship, grace without the cross.”

What we are advocating is not cheap grace, because:

**1. It insists that identity necessarily leads to action**

Children will surely want to be like their Father. This is not optional; it is nature.

**2. It insists on the cost of the cross**

This unconditional love cost the highest price: the life of the Son of God.

**3. It insists on the call to discipleship**

Following Christ is a real calling, but the motive is not to earn an identity; it is to live one out.

**True grace:** freely giving the identity of a child, and then the child naturally wanting to be like the Father.

### Misunderstanding 2: “Won’t this lead to license?”

**Romans 6:1–2**

> “Shall we go on sinning so that grace may increase? By no means! We are those who have died to sin; how can we live in it any longer?”

Paul’s logic:

- ❌ Not: because of grace, you are not allowed to keep sinning (a prohibition)
- ✓ But: you have already died to sin, so how could you go on living in it? (an impossibility of being)

**An analogy: the fish and the water**

A fish says to the water, “Can I leave you and live on land?” The water answers, “You can try, but that isn’t life; it’s death. You are a fish; your nature needs water.”

In the same way:

Someone in Christ asks, “Can I keep living in sin?” The answer: “You can try, but it goes against your new nature. You are God’s child; your nature longs for the Father.”

**1 John 3:9**

> “No one who is born of God will continue to sin… they cannot go on sinning, because they have been born of God.”

What “cannot” means:

- ❌ Not: “not permitted” (an external prohibition)
- ✓ But: “against their nature” (an inner impossibility)

Just as an apple tree “cannot” bear oranges: not because it is forbidden, but because of its nature.

### Misunderstanding 3: “Isn’t this passivity?”

**Passivity says:** “God will do everything; I just need to wait.”

**We say:** “God has already made you a new creation; now **live it out**.”

**Philippians 2:12–13**

> “Continue to work out your salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you to will and to act.”

“Work out” ≠ “earn”

A better way to put it: “live out the reality of your salvation.”

**An analogy:**

A prince is sent by the king, his father, to study in a distant land. The king says, “Be a prince worthy of who you are.”

- ❌ He does not mean, “Try hard to become a prince” (he already is one)
- ✓ He means, “Live out your identity as a prince”

In the same way, God says to us: “You already are my child. Live it out.”

This takes:

- daily choices
- ongoing practice
- concrete action

But the foundation is: I already am a child (not trying to become one).

## 12. Facing Real Struggles

### When Change Is Slow or Invisible

This is the most painful question.

**Reframing the question:**

| The wrong question | The right question |
|---|---|
| “Have I changed?” | “Who am I?” |
| (centered on results) | (centered on identity) |

**The answer:**

> “Even if I failed again today, I am still my Father’s child.
>
> This struggle does not define me. Christ’s righteousness defines me.
>
> I hate this sin, not because I am afraid of punishment, but because it is not who I really am.
>
> Change may be slow, but its direction is certain, because the work of the Spirit is certain.”

**Paul’s example:**

**Romans 7:24**

> “What a wretched man I am! Who will rescue me from this body that is subject to death?”

This is the apostle Paul, who wrote so much of Scripture, and he is still struggling!

But chapter 8 begins:

> “Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.”

Paul’s struggle does not change his identity in Christ.

**Philippians 1:6**

> “He who began a good work in you will carry it on to completion.”

The focus is not on my effort but on the good work he began.

### When Outside Help Is Needed

**An important note:**

The relational framework does not say, “All you need is prayer; you don’t need any other help.”

God uses many tools:

- counseling
- medical care (including mental health care)
- the support of the church
- concrete strategies and accountability

Seeking help is not a sign of weak faith; it is a sign of wisdom.

And while seeking help, remember: my identity does not rest on “whether I can overcome this,” but on “I am God’s child.”

## 13. Practical Wisdom

### Re-centering Every Day

**A morning prayer:**

```
“Father,

Remind me again today of the truth:
I am your child, and this will not change.
Christ lives in me.
The Spirit has already given me a new nature.

Today, help me remember who I am,
to live as the person I already am,
not to strain to become someone I am not.

When I am weak, remind me: I am still your child.
When I grow, let me know: this is your grace.”
```

**An evening reflection:**

```
“Father,

Today I saw my weakness: (confess specifically)
I also saw your grace: (give thanks specifically)

Thank you that my identity does not rest on today’s performance,
but on the righteousness of Christ.

I am still your child.
Tomorrow, help me keep living out this identity.”
```

### When You Fail

**Functional thinking:**

```
I failed again → I haven’t changed → is the Spirit’s work not working? → am I even saved?
```

(A downward spiral)

**Relational thinking:**

```
I failed again → but I am still my Father’s child → I go back to him →
his love has not changed → I stand up again in his grace
```

(Back to assurance)

### Changing the Language of Relationships

**With children / a spouse:**

- ❌ “I love you, but you need to…”
- ✓ “I love you, no matter what. Because I love you, I will guide you, but my love doesn’t depend on your performance.”

**In teaching:**

- ❌ “You must strive to become holy”
- ✓ “You are already in Christ; live out your true identity”

- ❌ “The Spirit can enable you to overcome sin”
- ✓ “The Spirit has already made you free; you are no longer a slave to sin”

### Self-Examination

Ask yourself these questions:

**1. When I think “God loves me,” what is the very next thought?**

- ❌ “So I need to try harder…” (a sense of obligation)
- ✓ “I am his child!” (wonder and rest)

**2. How do I see failure?**

- ❌ “I’ve let God down again”
- ✓ “I am still his child; I will go back to the Father’s arms”

**3. Where does my motivation come from?**

- ❌ Fear (if I don’t change…)
- ✓ Love (because I am loved, I want to respond)

**4. Where do my expectations of my children / others come from?**

- ❌ My investment needs a return
- ✓ I care about their good

## 14. Complete Examples in Real Situations

### Scenario 1: A teenager says, “I don’t want to go to church anymore”

**Functional response:**

“We’ve taken you to church all these years and prayed for you, all because we want you to be a godly person. How can you do this? You need to repent and let God change your heart.”

**Message conveyed:** our love is an investment that expects a return; you have let us down

**Relational response:**

“Thank you for telling me honestly. I want to understand what’s going on. (Listening.)

I love you. Whether or not you go to church, you are my child, and that will never change.

But I also want to tell you why church matters so much to me. It isn’t because of rules; it’s because that’s where I have experienced God’s love. I hope you can experience it too, not to meet my expectations, but because it is truly good.

You know what? God loves you whether or not you go to church. But his heart is for you to know him and experience fellowship. That isn’t a burden; it’s a blessing.

Can we talk about what you’re struggling with?”

**Message conveyed:** love is unconditional; I care about your struggle itself; an invitation to talk

### Scenario 2: A spouse’s repeated failure

**Functional response:**

“How many times have I forgiven you already? Why are you still like this? You need to repent seriously and let God change you!”

**Message conveyed:** my forgiveness has limits; your change is the condition of my continuing to love you

**Relational response:**

“I know you’ve struggled again. I’ve been through struggles like this too.

I want you to know: I love you, and I choose to forgive you, and that won’t change. Not because you’ve already fixed yourself, but because you are my spouse and we are committed to each other in the covenant of marriage.

I know this pattern causes you pain, and it has hurt our relationship too. I want to face this struggle with you, not as your judge, but as your partner.

Would you be willing to talk with me about what the real struggle underneath this is? Maybe we can pray together and look for help together?”

**Message conveyed:** love is covenantal; I am with you in your struggle; we face it together

## Conclusion: A Fundamental Choice

Every one of us, every day, is choosing between these two frameworks:

**Framework A asks:** “Have I gotten better? Am I doing enough? Have I measured up?” → Focus: my performance → Result: anxiety, performance, burnout

**Framework B asks:** “Who am I? Who loves me? How do I want to respond?” → Focus: my identity and relationship → Result: freedom, authenticity, joy

This is not just an academic fine point. It touches how you experience God, how you live out your faith, and how you love the people around you.

When you stop making “change” the goal, when you begin to rest in the assurance of “being loved,” you will discover:

**The person you have been trying so hard to become, in God’s eyes, you already are: you are his child.**

**Now simply live as the person you truly are.**

Change will happen, but as the natural result of growing up in the Father’s arms, not as the price of admission to the Father’s love.

---

The triune God is complete and joyful in eternal fellowship. He loves you not to get something from you, but to share his joy with you.

**This love is an end in itself. You are an end in yourself.**

Here is a subtle but crucial distinction:

**Does “change” matter most, or does the “person”?**

The difference looks small, but it goes to the heart of things.

Get it right, and love is love. Get it wrong, and love becomes a tool for reaching a goal.

The paradox is this:

When change no longer takes first place, when real love is poured out, change comes as fruit, all on its own.

When you truly believe it, that you are loved not because you will get better but simply because you are you,

**everything will be different.**
