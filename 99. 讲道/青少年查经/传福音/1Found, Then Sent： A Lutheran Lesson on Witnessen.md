# Found, Then Sent: A Lutheran Lesson on Witness

Nov 8, 2026 · @Jian Zhang

## Overview

**Central idea:** People who have been found by Jesus tell others, "Come and see him." The Holy Spirit creates faith through the Word; the results are his, not ours.

This lesson covers the same ground as Course 7, Lesson 1 ("Opportunities to Witness"), including both key points. It changes the order: first what Jesus has done for us, then what we do. Part 2 uses Jacob's well and the Old Testament "meeting at the well" stories to bring out the Gospel's central question: Who is Jesus?

**Goals.** By the end, students should be able to:

1. Say why witness begins with being found by Jesus, not with technique.
2. Explain how John uses Jacob's well and the Old Testament well stories to answer "Who is Jesus?"
3. Explain why rejection is not failure, using Luke 10:16 and 1 Corinthians 3:6-7.
4. Name one place God has put them and one way they take part in the church's mission.

## Part 1. Opening: naming our fears (5 min)

_\[Open with a short prayer. Then write on the whiteboard: "What scares me about talking to friends about Jesus?"\]_

Let's start with an honest question. Imagine you're at lunch with a friend who doesn't go to church, and the conversation drifts toward God. What's the thing that makes you want to change the subject?

You don't need a "right" answer. I want the real one.

_\[Wait, and write every answer on the board.\]_

_\[If nobody speaks, offer one of your own:\]_ I'll go first. When I talk about my faith, my biggest worry is that the other person will brush me off, and then I'll feel like I failed.

_\[Draw a box around two kinds of answers if they come up, or add them yourself: (1) fear of being rejected, and (2) feeling responsible for whether the person believes.\]_

Thank you for being honest. Keep these on the board. We're going to come back to them, and I think by the end of today, a couple of them will look different.

But we're not going to start with what we should do. We're going to start with someone who was even more nervous about people than we are.

## Part 2. Found first: John 4:4-26 (20 min)

_\[Have students read John 4:4-26 aloud, switching readers at verses 4, 10, 16, and 21.\]_

**Who came looking for whom?**

Look at verse 4: Jesus "had to" go through Samaria. Strictly speaking, he didn't. Many Jews traveling between Judea and Galilee went around Samaria to avoid it. In John's Gospel, "had to" usually means God's plan. Jesus went there because someone was waiting at that well who needed him.

Now look at verses 5-6. John is careful to tell us where this happens: Sychar, "near the plot of ground Jacob had given to his son Joseph. Jacob's well was there." He also tells us when: it was about noon. Why would someone draw water in the hottest part of the day?

So here is a woman who is avoiding people. She's a Samaritan, and Jews and Samaritans didn't associate (v. 9). And Jesus walks right up to her.

**A scene at a well.** Anyone who knows the Old Testament hears "a man meets a woman at a well" and thinks of a few stories. Abraham's servant found Rebekah at a well, as a bride for Isaac (Genesis 24). Jacob met Rachel at a well (Genesis 29:1-12). Moses met Zipporah at a well in Midian (Exodus 2:15-21). These stories run the same way: a man travels to a foreign land, meets a woman at a well, someone draws water, the woman runs home with the news, the man is invited to stay, and a marriage follows.

John 4 matches almost every step. Jesus is in a foreign land, sitting at a well, and not just any well, but Jacob's. He meets a woman, they talk about drawing water, she runs back to town with the news, and the town invites him to stay (v. 40). But the ending is different. There's no wedding. Instead, the subject of husbands comes up (v. 16-18).

Why tell it this way? Just one chapter earlier, John the Baptist called Jesus "the bridegroom" (John 3:29). The prophets often pictured God and his people as a marriage, and the people's turning away from God as unfaithfulness (Hosea 2:16-20). John is hinting that the true Bridegroom has come to the well, and the bride he is looking for is one who doesn't deserve him.

**Ask:** Jacob found Rachel at the well. At Jacob's well, who is Jesus looking for?

This isn't the first time God has come looking like this. What was the first thing God did after Adam and Eve sinned? They hid, and God came looking for them: "Where are you?" (Genesis 3:9). From the Garden of Eden to the well at Sychar, God is the one who comes looking first.

**Greater than Jacob? (v. 10-14).** Jesus says, "If you knew the gift of God and who it is that asks you for a drink." Notice the two things he wants her to know: the gift, and who he is. That's what this whole conversation is about.

She doesn't understand, and pushes back: "Are you greater than our father Jacob, who gave us the well?" (v. 12). Samaritans saw themselves as Jacob's descendants. This well was her pride, a sign of her people and her heritage.

**Ask:** What answer does she expect? How does John want us, the readers, to answer?

She assumes the answer is obviously "no." The reader knows it's "yes." John has already put Jesus next to Jacob. In 1:51, Jesus tells Nathanael, "You will see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man." That is Jacob's ladder from his dream at Bethel (Genesis 28:12). Jesus himself is the ladder, the place where heaven and earth meet.

Whoever drinks from Jacob's well gets thirsty again. The water Jesus gives "will become in them a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (v. 14). In the Old Testament, "living water" is a picture of God himself. God says of his people, "They have forsaken me, the spring of living water, and have dug their own cisterns, broken cisterns that cannot hold water" (Jeremiah 2:13). So Jesus isn't just greater than Jacob. He gives water that only God can give. The living water is a gift. He doesn't sell it, and she doesn't earn it.

**The hard part (v. 16-18).** Then Jesus says something uncomfortable: "Go, call your husband." Why would he bring that up?

Here's what I find amazing. Jesus knows everything she has ever done, every husband, the man she's living with now, all of it. And he's still talking to her. He didn't find out and leave. He knew it all before he sat down at that well.

**Who is he? (v. 19-26).** Watch how her picture of Jesus grows. First he's just "a Jew" (v. 9). Then she asks if he's greater than Jacob (v. 12). Then "a prophet" (v. 19). Finally, Jesus tells her plainly: "I, the one speaking to you, am he." He is the Messiah.

That is exactly why John wrote his Gospel: "these are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God" (John 20:31). The whole Gospel of John answers one question: Who is Jesus? This conversation at the well is that question in miniature. A woman goes from "you are a Jew" to "could this be the Messiah?"

**Discuss:** Jesus knew everything about her and still came looking for her. Does that feel scary or comforting? Why?

_\[Let several students answer. Then bring it home:\]_

Sometimes people read this story mainly as a lesson in how to start conversations. But we can't do what he did. We can't see into someone's past. This story isn't first about how to talk to people. It's about who Jesus is and what he gives.

And here's the point for us. In God's eyes, we are all that Samaritan woman. Every one of us was found by Jesus before we ever looked for him. Luther's Small Catechism says it like this: "I believe that I cannot by my own reason or strength believe in Jesus Christ, my Lord, or come to him; but the Holy Spirit has called me by the gospel."

Everything we'll talk about today starts there. You aren't a salesperson for Jesus. You're someone he found.

## Part 3. Come and see: John 4:27-42 (15 min)

_\[Have students read John 4:27-42 aloud. Point out that the workbook stops at verse 15; this is the part where the witnessing actually happens.\]_

**What did she say? (v. 28-29).** Look at what she does first: she leaves her water jar. She came for water and forgot about it. Then she goes back into town, to the same people she was avoiding at noon, and says: "Come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Could this be the Christ?"

Let's look at that sentence closely. Did she give a speech? Did she explain the whole Bible? Was she even sure?

That's witness. Not a sales pitch. One person who was found by Jesus, saying, "Come and see him." Philip said almost the same words to Nathanael back in chapter 1.

**Who made them believe? (v. 39-42).** Verse 39 says many believed because of her testimony. But look at verse 42. The townspeople tell her: "We no longer believe just because of what you said; now we have heard for ourselves."

Is that an insult to her?

**Back to the board.** _\[Point to the two boxed fears.\]_ Now let's look again at what we wrote at the beginning.

First: "I feel responsible for whether they believe." Who made the people of Sychar believe? Not the woman. Jesus did, through his word. Paul says, "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Corinthians 3:6-7). Our Lutheran confessions say the Holy Spirit works faith "where and when it pleases God" in those who hear the gospel (Augsburg Confession, Article V). Your job is to speak. The result is not on your shoulders.

Second: "What if they reject me?" Jesus said, "Whoever rejects you rejects me" (Luke 10:16). If a friend says no, they aren't rejecting you. And Jesus was rejected too: "He came to his own, and his own did not receive him" (John 1:11).

_\[Tell your short story here, if you prepared one: a time you spoke about your faith and it didn't go well, and what God taught you through it.\]_

**Discuss:** If you talk to a friend about Jesus and they say no, did you fail?

One caution. "The results are God's" doesn't mean "so I don't need to say anything." It means you can say something without carrying a weight that isn't yours.

**Discuss:** If I don't share the gospel, is that a sin?

According to the law, yes. The law tells us to love our neighbor. If God puts someone in front of me who needs the gospel, and I stay silent because I'm afraid or embarrassed, I'm not loving that person (James 4:17). But by that same standard, every one of us is guilty. None of us witnesses enough, or boldly enough, or faithfully enough. That calls for repentance, and it has already been forgiven at the cross.

But be careful: "sharing the gospel" can't be shrunk down to one kind of activity. The Lutheran theologian Kenneth Cherney tells about something that happened when he was a vicar. In his congregation was a middle-aged woman whose whole life showed Christ's love. She was the most faithful church secretary you could imagine. She served in several church organizations. She had raised three daughters who were all capable Christians in their fields. Her home was warm and welcoming, and nobody ever left it hungry.

One day several churches brought in an energetic speaker to talk about personal evangelism. Afterward, everyone went out excitedly to knock on doors, and she went too. When they came back to share, the results were what you'd expect: a few people were welcoming, most politely said no, and one or two were openly hostile. But what bothered her wasn't the rejection. It was realizing that, in middle age, this was the first time she had ever done anything like this. She told him she felt she had been wasting her life. Cherney sensed something was wrong with that, but at the time he couldn't say what.

What do you think? Had she been wasting her life?

Cherney later came to see the problem. He calls it "evangelism reductionism": taking all the countless ways God tells us to love people and shrinking them into one command, "do personal evangelism," as if that were the whole Christian life. He points out that Scripture never makes personal evangelism the one thing a Christian life can't do without. For decades this woman had cared for her family, served her church, and welcomed guests. She was loving her neighbors in the callings God gave her. None of it was wasted.

God has put you in a family, a school, a team. Those are your callings. Honoring your parents, working hard in class, sitting with a friend who's hurting, doing the job nobody else wants to do: these are ways God has called you to love people. They aren't a waste of your life. And it's exactly in those relationships, when a friend needs hope, that you'll have the most natural chance to talk about Jesus.

So we don't share the gospel to escape the charge "not witnessing is a sin." Cherney draws on the theologian J. P. Koehler, who said, in effect, that a heart won over by Christ's love knows there's no better way to serve the world than to share that good news. People who have been found want to tell others, "Come and see him."

**Where does this happen? (Key Point 1).** The woman didn't travel to a mission field. She went back to her own town. God has put you somewhere too: your family, your school, your team, your friends. That's your place.

Read 1 Peter 3:15: "Always be prepared to give an answer to everyone who asks you to give the reason for the hope that you have. But do this with gentleness and respect."

**Agree or disagree:** "As long as I live a good Christian life, I don't have to talk about my faith."

**Agree or disagree:** "Talking about faith makes people uncomfortable, so it's better to wait until they ask."

## Part 4. Sent together: Matthew 9:36-10:8, 10:14 (10 min)

**God sent himself first.** Before we talk about how the church is sent, we need to know that sending starts with God. Theologians call this _missio Dei_, "the mission of God." Mission isn't first the church's project. It's God's own work. The Father sent the Son into the world (John 3:16-17). The Son sends the Spirit (John 15:26). Then Jesus tells his disciples, "As the Father has sent me, I am sending you" (John 20:21).

God didn't say, "Okay, Jesus died on the cross. The rest is up to you." When Jesus sent his disciples, he promised, "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (Matthew 28:20). He also said the Spirit would testify about him, "and you also must testify" (John 15:26-27). When we speak, the Holy Spirit is already at work.

So think again about those fears on the board. You are not fighting alone. You're joining work God has been doing all along, and is still doing today.

_\[Have a student read Matthew 9:36-38.\]_

**It starts with compassion and prayer.** Jesus saw the crowds and had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd. Then what's the first thing he tells his disciples to do? Not "go." He says, "Ask the Lord of the harvest to send out workers." Mission starts with prayer, because the harvest is God's.

_\[Have a student read Matthew 10:5-8, then 10:14.\]_

**Jesus expected rejection.** In chapter 10, Jesus sends out the Twelve. Look at verse 14: "If anyone will not welcome you or listen to your words, shake the dust off your feet." Jesus told them ahead of time that some people would say no. And he told them not to carry that with them. Shake it off and move on.

These twelve were apostles, sent with a special office and special power. Most of us aren't called to be pastors or missionaries. But the whole church shares in sending them, and in the work they do.

**Agree or disagree:** "Evangelism programs at church are how members help the pastor do his job."

**How do we take part?** _\[Have a student read Philippians 4:14-16.\]_ The church in Philippi supported Paul's mission with gifts. Our congregation and our synod send pastors, teachers, and missionaries at home and around the world. We share in their work through our offerings and our prayers.

And here's one you already do every week. When we pray "Your kingdom come" in the Lord's Prayer, we are praying for missions. The Small Catechism explains: God's kingdom certainly comes by itself even without our prayer, but we pray in this petition that it may also come to us. And we pray it would come to others too.

Another way to take part is simple: inviting a friend to church or youth group. Not to boost attendance, but to bring them to the place where the Word is preached and the sacraments are given. That's what the woman did. She brought people to Jesus.

**Homework:** _\[Point to the back of the handout.\]_ Before next week, find out what our church is doing in outreach this year. Ask the pastor or someone on the evangelism committee. We'll share what we learned next time.

## Closing (3 min)

_\[Point back to the board one last time.\]_

We started today with the things that scare us. Those fears are real, and they won't all disappear. But remember what we saw. The woman at the well was the last person you'd expect to be a witness. She didn't have a script or all the answers. She had been found by Jesus, so she said, "Come and see." And the people who believed ended up resting on his word, not hers.

That's all God asks of you. Not to win arguments. Not to make anyone believe. Just to tell people about the one who found you.

On your handout there's a question to think about this week: "Who has God put around me?" You don't have to share your answer with anyone. Just think about it, and pray for those people by name.

_\[Close by praying the Lord's Prayer together, pausing briefly after "Your kingdom come."\]_

## Teacher background: Jacob's well (you don't need to teach all of this)

This section is for your own preparation, in case students ask.

**The Old Testament never says Jacob dug a well.** "Jacob's well" is a Jewish and Samaritan tradition, and John's Gospel is the earliest surviving text to mention it. The plot of land, though, is clearly in the Old Testament:

- Coming back from Paddan Aram, Jacob camped in front of the city of Shechem, bought the land from the sons of Hamor, and built an altar there called El Elohe Israel (Genesis 33:18-20).
- On his deathbed Jacob gave Joseph one portion more than his brothers (Genesis 48:22). The Hebrew word for "portion" is *shekem*, the same as the name Shechem, a deliberate pun.
- The Israelites carried Joseph's bones out of Egypt and buried them at Shechem, in the plot Jacob had bought (Joshua 24:32). Joshua also gathered the people at Shechem to renew the covenant (Joshua 24).

Going further back, Shechem was the first place Abraham stopped in Canaan. God first promised the land to his offspring there, and Abraham built an altar (Genesis 12:6-7). The "mountain" the woman mentions (v. 20) is Mount Gerizim, right next to Shechem, where Moses commanded the blessings to be proclaimed (Deuteronomy 11:29; 27:12). So Jesus is sitting at the center of the patriarchs' story. When she asks whether to worship on this mountain or in Jerusalem, all of that history is behind the question. Jesus' answer (v. 21-24) goes beyond both mountains: true worship isn't about which mountain.

**The "five husbands" as a symbol.** Some interpreters read the five husbands as a picture of Samaritan history: the king of Assyria resettled people from five places in Samaria, each bringing its own gods (2 Kings 17:24-41). The reading has something to it, but 2 Kings 17 actually lists more than five gods, and John doesn't say so. Mention it as "some read it this way," not as settled.

**More Old Testament background on living water.** Jeremiah 17:13; Isaiah 55:1 ("Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters"); Isaiah 12:3 ("With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation"); Ezekiel 47 and Zechariah 14:8, where living water flows from the temple and Jerusalem. She is thinking of well water that would save her the trip; Jesus means the water the prophets promised, which only God can give.

**An echo inside John (good for older students).** Jesus sits at the well "about noon" and asks for a drink. The next time John says "about noon" is when Pilate hands Jesus over (19:14). Soon after, on the cross, Jesus says, "I am thirsty" (19:28). The one who asked her for water at the well later thirsted on the cross, for her and for us.

## Answer key for teachers

This section is for the teacher only; don't print it on the student handout. The answers are suggestions. In discussion, let students answer first.

**1. Found first**

- **What was the first thing God did after Adam and Eve sinned?** They hid, and God came looking for them: "Where are you?" (Genesis 3:9). From the beginning, God is the one who comes looking first.
- **Who went looking for whom?** Jesus went looking for her. He "had to" go through Samaria (v. 4), and that "had to" is God's plan. She came for water at noon because she was avoiding people.
- **Who met his future wife at a well? What does John the Baptist call Jesus?** Abraham's servant found Rebekah for Isaac, Jacob met Rachel, and Moses met Zipporah. John the Baptist calls Jesus "the bridegroom" (John 3:29). The true Bridegroom comes to Jacob's well, looking for a bride who doesn't deserve him.
- **What two things does Jesus want her to know?** The gift of God (the living water), and who he is (the man asking her for a drink is the one who gives living water).
- **"Are you greater than our father Jacob?" How does John want us to answer?** She expects "no"; the reader knows it's "yes." Jesus is the ladder from Jacob's dream, where heaven and earth meet (John 1:51; Genesis 28:12). Whoever drinks from Jacob's well gets thirsty again, but Jesus' water becomes a spring welling up to eternal life; and in the Old Testament, the spring of living water is God himself (Jeremiah 2:13). So he gives water only God can give.
- **Why does Jesus bring up her husbands?**
  1. To show her that he knows her completely, including what she most wants to hide, and is still talking to her.
  2. To bring her real thirst into the open: she is thinking about well water (v. 15), but Jesus is dealing with her life. Law first, then gospel.
  3. To reveal who he is: it leads her to say "I can see that you are a prophet" (v. 19), and later, "He told me everything I ever did" (v. 29).
  4. To echo the well stories: the others end in a wedding, but here the subject of husbands comes up.

  Note: the text doesn't say why she had five husbands. She may have been widowed, or divorced by them. Don't present her as "a really bad woman." The point is that Jesus knows all of it, and he came for her.

  Short answer: "Not to shame her, but to show her that Jesus knows her completely and still wants to give her living water. It also helps her see who Jesus is."
- **How does her view of Jesus change? What does John want us to believe?** "You are a Jew" (v. 9) → "Are you greater than Jacob?" (v. 12) → "a prophet" (v. 19) → "I, the one speaking to you, am he" (v. 26); finally the town says, "this man really is the Savior of the world" (v. 42). John wrote his Gospel so that we may believe that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God, and by believing have life in his name (John 20:31).

**2. Come and see**

- **What did the woman say to the town? How sure was she?** "Come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Could this be the Messiah?" (v. 29). She wasn't completely sure. A witness doesn't need to know everything; she only needs to bring people to Jesus: "Come and see."
- **Whose word did the townspeople finally believe?** Jesus' own word: "We no longer believe just because of what you said; now we have heard for ourselves" (v. 42). That doesn't put her down. It's exactly the goal of witness: to bring people to Jesus so their faith rests on his word.
- **"As long as I live a good Christian life, I don't have to talk about my faith."** Not entirely. Life matters (1 Peter 2:12; 3:1-2), and loving people in our callings is not wasted. But a life by itself can't say who Jesus is: "faith comes from hearing the message" (Romans 10:14, 17). And our lives aren't perfect; the gospel isn't about how good I am, but about Jesus forgiving sinners. Takeaway: "My life opens the door; my words point to Jesus."
- **"Talking about faith makes people uncomfortable, so it's better to wait until they ask."** Half true. Gentleness, respect, and good timing are right, and 1 Peter 3:15 does speak of when someone asks. But the discomfort is often ours; people often don't know you're a Christian, so they never ask; and the gospel will make some people uncomfortable anyway (1 Corinthians 1:23). "Always be prepared" isn't passive waiting. You can open the door naturally, for example by saying "I'll pray for you," or inviting a friend to youth group. Takeaway: "Don't force it, but be ready."

**Fear check: If a friend says no, did I fail?** No.

- **1 Corinthians 3:6-7:** God gives the growth. We plant and water; the results are his.
- **Luke 10:16:** People are really rejecting Jesus, and the Father who sent him, not me.
- **1 Peter 3:15:** Always be prepared, and answer with gentleness and respect.
- **Is not sharing the gospel a sin? Is ordinary love and service a waste of life?** By the law, if God puts someone who needs the gospel in front of me and I say nothing out of fear or pride, I haven't loved them (James 4:17). But everyone falls short here; that calls for repentance, and it has been forgiven at the cross. At the same time, loving and serving people at home and at school is God's calling, not a waste of life (Cherney's story of the church member). And those relationships are where we most naturally get to talk about Jesus.

**3. Sent together**

- **Where does sending start?** With God: the Father sent the Son, the Son sends the Spirit, and Jesus sends his disciples: "As the Father has sent me, I am sending you" (John 20:21). Mission is first God's work.
- **Who is with us when we share the gospel?** Jesus: "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (Matthew 28:20). And the Holy Spirit testifies about him (John 15:26-27).
- **What does Jesus tell his disciples to do first?** Pray: "Ask the Lord of the harvest to send out workers into his harvest field" (9:38). The harvest is God's.
- **What did Jesus say to do when people don't listen?** Shake the dust off your feet and keep going (10:14). Jesus expected rejection; we don't have to carry it around.
- **"Evangelism programs at church are how members help the pastor do his job."** Disagree. Mission belongs to the whole church, not to the pastor alone. Pastors and missionaries have a special office, and the congregation shares in their work by sending, giving, and praying (Philippians 4:14-16); every believer also witnesses and invites friends in their own calling.
- **What are we praying for when we say "Your kingdom come"?** As the Small Catechism explains: God's kingdom comes even without our prayer, but we pray that it may come to us also; that is, that our heavenly Father gives us his Holy Spirit, so that by his grace we believe his Word and lead godly lives. We also pray that it comes to more people, which is praying for mission.

**To think about this week:** There's no right answer; it's just for the student.
