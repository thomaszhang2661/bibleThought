# Found, Then Sent: A Lutheran Lesson on Witness

Nov 8, 2026 · @Jian Zhang

## Overview

**Central idea:** People who have been found by Jesus tell others, "Come and see him." The Holy Spirit creates faith through the Word; the results are his, not ours.

This lesson covers the same ground as Course 7, Lesson 1 ("Opportunities to Witness"), including both key points. It changes the order: first what Jesus has done for us, then what we do.

**Goals.** By the end, students should be able to:

1. Say why witness begins with being found by Jesus, not with technique.
2. Explain why rejection is not failure, using Luke 10:16 and 1 Corinthians 3:6-7.
3. Name one place God has put them and one way they take part in the church's mission.

## Part 1. Opening: naming our fears (5 min)

_\[Open with a short prayer. Then write on the whiteboard: "What scares me about talking to friends about Jesus?"\]_

Let's start with an honest question. Imagine you're at lunch with a friend who doesn't go to church, and the conversation drifts toward God. What's the thing that makes you want to change the subject?

You don't need a "right" answer. I want the real one.

_\[Wait, and write every answer on the board.\]_

_\[If nobody speaks, offer one of your own:\]_ I'll go first. When I talk about my faith, my biggest worry is that the other person will brush me off, and then I'll feel like I failed.

_\[Draw a box around two kinds of answers if they come up, or add them yourself: (1) fear of being rejected, and (2) feeling responsible for whether the person believes.\]_

Thank you for being honest. Keep these on the board. We're going to come back to them, and I think by the end of today, a couple of them will look different.

But we're not going to start with what we should do. We're going to start with someone who was even more nervous about people than we are.

## Part 2. Found first: John 4:4-26 (15 min)

_\[Have students read John 4:4-26 aloud, switching readers at verses 4, 10, 16, and 21.\]_

**Who came looking for whom?**

Look at verse 4: Jesus "had to" go through Samaria. Strictly speaking, he didn't. Many Jews traveling between Judea and Galilee went around Samaria to avoid it. In John's Gospel, "had to" usually means God's plan. Jesus went there because someone was waiting at that well who needed him.

Now look at verse 6: it was about noon. Why would someone draw water in the hottest part of the day?

So here is a woman who is avoiding people. She's a Samaritan, and Jews and Samaritans didn't associate (v. 9). And Jesus walks right up to her.

This isn't the first time God has done this. What was the first thing God did after Adam and Eve sinned? They hid, and God came looking for them: "Where are you?" (Genesis 3:9). From the Garden of Eden to the well at Sychar, God is the one who comes looking first.

**The gift (v. 10-14).** Jesus says, "If you knew the gift of God and who it is that asks you for a drink." Notice the two things he wants her to know: the gift, and who he is. That's what this whole conversation is about. The living water is a gift. He doesn't sell it, and she doesn't earn it.

**The hard part (v. 16-18).** Then Jesus says something uncomfortable: "Go, call your husband." Why would he bring that up?

Here's what I find amazing. Jesus knows everything she has ever done, every husband, the man she's living with now, all of it. And he's still talking to her. He didn't find out and leave. He knew it all before he sat down at that well.

**Who is he? (v. 19-26).** Watch how her picture of Jesus grows. First he's just "a Jew" (v. 9). Then she asks if he's greater than Jacob (v. 12). Then "a prophet" (v. 19). Finally, Jesus tells her plainly: "I, the one speaking to you, am he." He is the Messiah.

**Discuss:** Jesus knew everything about her and still came looking for her. Does that feel scary or comforting? Why?

_\[Let several students answer. Then bring it home:\]_

Sometimes people read this story mainly as a lesson in how to start conversations. But we can't do what he did. We can't see into someone's past. This story isn't first about how to talk to people. It's about who Jesus is and what he gives.

And here's the point for us. In God's eyes, we are all that Samaritan woman. Every one of us was found by Jesus before we ever looked for him. Luther's Small Catechism says it like this: "I believe that I cannot by my own reason or strength believe in Jesus Christ, my Lord, or come to him; but the Holy Spirit has called me by the gospel."

Everything we'll talk about today starts there. You aren't a salesperson for Jesus. You're someone he found.

## Part 3. Come and see: John 4:27-42 (15 min)

_\[Have students read John 4:27-42 aloud. Point out that the workbook stops at verse 15; this is the part where the witnessing actually happens.\]_

**What did she say? (v. 28-29).** Look at what she does first: she leaves her water jar. She came for water and forgot about it. Then she goes back into town, to the same people she was avoiding at noon, and says: "Come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Could this be the Christ?"

Let's look at that sentence closely. Did she give a speech? Did she explain the whole Bible? Was she even sure?

That's witness. Not a sales pitch. One person who was found by Jesus, saying, "Come and see him." Philip said almost the same words to Nathanael back in chapter 1.

**Who made them believe? (v. 39-42).** Verse 39 says many believed because of her testimony. But look at verse 42. The townspeople tell her: "We no longer believe just because of what you said; now we have heard for ourselves."

Is that an insult to her?

**Back to the board.** _\[Point to the two boxed fears.\]_ Now let's look again at what we wrote at the beginning.

First: "I feel responsible for whether they believe." Who made the people of Sychar believe? Not the woman. Jesus did, through his word. Paul says, "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Corinthians 3:6-7). Our Lutheran confessions say the Holy Spirit works faith "where and when it pleases God" in those who hear the gospel (Augsburg Confession, Article V). Your job is to speak. The result is not on your shoulders.

Second: "What if they reject me?" Jesus said, "Whoever rejects you rejects me" (Luke 10:16). If a friend says no, they aren't rejecting you. And Jesus was rejected too: "He came to his own, and his own did not receive him" (John 1:11).

_\[Tell your short story here, if you prepared one: a time you spoke about your faith and it didn't go well, and what God taught you through it.\]_

**Discuss:** If you talk to a friend about Jesus and they say no, did you fail?

One caution. "The results are God's" doesn't mean "so I don't need to say anything." It means you can say something without carrying a weight that isn't yours.

**Discuss:** If I don't share the gospel, is that a sin?

According to the law, yes. The law tells us to love our neighbor. If God puts someone in front of me who needs the gospel, and I stay silent because I'm afraid or embarrassed, I'm not loving that person (James 4:17). But by that same standard, every one of us is guilty. None of us witnesses enough, or boldly enough, or faithfully enough. That calls for repentance, and it has already been forgiven at the cross.

But be careful: "sharing the gospel" can't be shrunk down to one kind of activity. The Lutheran theologian Kenneth Cherney tells about something that happened when he was a vicar. In his congregation was a middle-aged woman whose whole life showed Christ's love. She was the most faithful church secretary you could imagine. She served in several church organizations. She had raised three daughters who were all capable Christians in their fields. Her home was warm and welcoming, and nobody ever left it hungry.

One day several churches brought in an energetic speaker to talk about personal evangelism. Afterward, everyone went out excitedly to knock on doors, and she went too. When they came back to share, the results were what you'd expect: a few people were welcoming, most politely said no, and one or two were openly hostile. But what bothered her wasn't the rejection. It was realizing that, in middle age, this was the first time she had ever done anything like this. She told him she felt she had been wasting her life. Cherney sensed something was wrong with that, but at the time he couldn't say what.

What do you think? Had she been wasting her life?

Cherney later came to see the problem. He calls it "evangelism reductionism": taking all the countless ways God tells us to love people and shrinking them into one command, "do personal evangelism," as if that were the whole Christian life. He points out that Scripture never makes personal evangelism the one thing a Christian life can't do without. For decades this woman had cared for her family, served her church, and welcomed guests. She was loving her neighbors in the callings God gave her. None of it was wasted.

God has put you in a family, a school, a team. Those are your callings. Honoring your parents, working hard in class, sitting with a friend who's hurting, doing the job nobody else wants to do: these are ways God has called you to love people. They aren't a waste of your life. And it's exactly in those relationships, when a friend needs hope, that you'll have the most natural chance to talk about Jesus.

So we don't share the gospel to escape the charge "not witnessing is a sin." Cherney draws on the theologian J. P. Koehler, who said, in effect, that a heart won over by Christ's love knows there's no better way to serve the world than to share that good news. People who have been found want to tell others, "Come and see him."

**Where does this happen? (Key Point 1).** The woman didn't travel to a mission field. She went back to her own town. God has put you somewhere too: your family, your school, your team, your friends. That's your place.

Read 1 Peter 3:15: "Always be prepared to give an answer to everyone who asks you to give the reason for the hope that you have. But do this with gentleness and respect."

**Agree or disagree:** "As long as I live a good Christian life, I don't have to talk about my faith."

**Agree or disagree:** "Talking about faith makes people uncomfortable, so it's better to wait until they ask."

## Part 4. Sent together: Matthew 9:36-10:8, 10:14 (10 min)

**God sent himself first.** Before we talk about how the church is sent, we need to know that sending starts with God. Theologians call this _missio Dei_, "the mission of God." Mission isn't first the church's project. It's God's own work. The Father sent the Son into the world (John 3:16-17). The Son sends the Spirit (John 15:26). Then Jesus tells his disciples, "As the Father has sent me, I am sending you" (John 20:21).

God didn't say, "Okay, Jesus died on the cross. The rest is up to you." When Jesus sent his disciples, he promised, "I am with you always, to the very end of the age" (Matthew 28:20). He also said the Spirit would testify about him, "and you also must testify" (John 15:26-27). When we speak, the Holy Spirit is already at work.

So think again about those fears on the board. You are not fighting alone. You're joining work God has been doing all along, and is still doing today.

_\[Have a student read Matthew 9:36-38.\]_

**It starts with compassion and prayer.** Jesus saw the crowds and had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd. Then what's the first thing he tells his disciples to do? Not "go." He says, "Ask the Lord of the harvest to send out workers." Mission starts with prayer, because the harvest is God's.

_\[Have a student read Matthew 10:5-8, then 10:14.\]_

**Jesus expected rejection.** In chapter 10, Jesus sends out the Twelve. Look at verse 14: "If anyone will not welcome you or listen to your words, shake the dust off your feet." Jesus told them ahead of time that some people would say no. And he told them not to carry that with them. Shake it off and move on.

These twelve were apostles, sent with a special office and special power. Most of us aren't called to be pastors or missionaries. But the whole church shares in sending them, and in the work they do.

**Agree or disagree:** "Evangelism programs at church are how members help the pastor do his job."

**How do we take part?** _\[Have a student read Philippians 4:14-16.\]_ The church in Philippi supported Paul's mission with gifts. Our congregation and our synod send pastors, teachers, and missionaries at home and around the world. We share in their work through our offerings and our prayers.

And here's one you already do every week. When we pray "Your kingdom come" in the Lord's Prayer, we are praying for missions. The Small Catechism explains: God's kingdom certainly comes by itself even without our prayer, but we pray in this petition that it may also come to us. And we pray it would come to others too.

Another way to take part is simple: inviting a friend to church or youth group. Not to boost attendance, but to bring them to the place where the Word is preached and the sacraments are given. That's what the woman did. She brought people to Jesus.

**Homework:** _\[Point to the back of the handout.\]_ Before next week, find out what our church is doing in outreach this year. Ask the pastor or someone on the evangelism committee. We'll share what we learned next time.

## Closing (3 min)

_\[Point back to the board one last time.\]_

We started today with the things that scare us. Those fears are real, and they won't all disappear. But remember what we saw. The woman at the well was the last person you'd expect to be a witness. She didn't have a script or all the answers. She had been found by Jesus, so she said, "Come and see." And the people who believed ended up resting on his word, not hers.

That's all God asks of you. Not to win arguments. Not to make anyone believe. Just to tell people about the one who found you.

On your handout there's a question to think about this week: "Who has God put around me?" You don't have to share your answer with anyone. Just think about it, and pray for those people by name.

_\[Close by praying the Lord's Prayer together, pausing briefly after "Your kingdom come."\]_
