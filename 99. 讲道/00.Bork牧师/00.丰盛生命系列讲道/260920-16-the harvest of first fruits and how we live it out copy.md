

In September, we focused on the fruits of the Spirit displayed on these banners. We looked at each one, plus a few more that Scripture mentions but aren’t shown here. But September’s focus wasn’t on growing those fruits anymore—it was on harvesting them, and that’s what our service today is about: the harvest of first fruits and how we live it out. Let’s begin our worship with prayer. In Jesus’ name, Amen. Amen.

We’ll begin our worship with a song that reminds us of a promise once made but slow to be kept. Has that ever happened to you? The Corinthians had promised to help support the believers in Jerusalem who were going through a time of poverty. Paul was reminding them of that promise—and also of the blessing that would come, not only to the believers in Jerusalem through their offering, but to the Corinthians themselves through their giving. That’s right—he was telling them that by giving, they too would be blessed. What he pointed out is that God’s grace is abundantly at work through our giving from the harvest of blessings we’ve received. Let’s listen to these words Paul wrote in 2 Corinthians 9:

“Remember this: Whoever sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and whoever sows generously will also reap generously. Each of you should give what you have decided in your heart to give, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver. And God is able to bless you abundantly, so that in all things at all times, having all that you need, you will abound in every good work. As it is written: ‘They have freely scattered their gifts to the poor; their righteousness endures forever.’ Now he who supplies seed to the sower and bread for food will also supply and increase your store of seed and will enlarge the harvest of your righteousness. You will be enriched in every way so that you can be generous on every occasion, and through us your generosity will result in thanksgiving to God. This service that you perform is not only supplying the needs of the Lord’s people but is also overflowing in many expressions of thanks to God. Because of the service by which you have proved yourselves, others will praise God for the obedience that accompanies your confession of the gospel of Christ, and for your generosity in sharing with them and with everyone else. And in their prayers for you, their hearts will go out to you because of the surpassing grace God has given you. Thanks be to God for his indescribable gift!”

In the song we just sang, we talked about the heart of worship, and Jesus also spoke about our hearts. He said that where our treasure is, there our heart will be also. So he cautions us not to place our treasure in the earthly things of this life, but rather in the heavenly blessings he has for us—for that is life’s true security. Jesus’ words are recorded in Matthew 6:

“Do not store up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moths and vermin destroy, and where thieves break in and steal. But store up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where moths and vermin do not destroy, and where thieves do not break in and steal. For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also. The eye is the lamp of the body. If your eyes are healthy, your whole body will be full of light. But if your eyes are unhealthy, your whole body will be full of darkness. If then the light within you is darkness, how great is that darkness! No one can serve two masters. Either you will hate the one and love the other, or you will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money.”

With those words, Jesus reminds us that our greatest treasure is the gift of eternal life through him. Now, I know many of you here this morning are parents, and you have wonderful little treasures with you—your children, your grandchildren. Bring them to that treasure. Let them know about Jesus, their Savior. I think that’s maybe why you have them enrolled in our school—because that’s what we want them to know.

**(Sermon)**

In our house, we have several sinks, and at each sink there’s a bottle of liquid or foam soap. This week I noticed a new one, so I wanted to see what scent it was. Now, by “flavor”—well, you probably don’t know what that means unless you’ve had your mouth washed out with soap. It’s been a long time since that happened to me, so I just put some in my hand and smelled it. But the label on the bottle said “Afternoon Apple Picking.” I wondered what that would smell like. Since I did some apple picking as a kid, I thought maybe I’d recognize the scent. So I put some in my hand, held it up to my nose, and guess what it smelled like?

(Of course, it just smelled like ordinary soap.)

So why is it that sometimes we give something plain and ordinary a fancy name or title? Is it because we’re trying to create some interest in it, or maybe just add some sparkle by giving it a new name? Or maybe there’s something behind it we’ve just never thought about before. I think that’s true of the term “first fruits.” It’s a term found in the Bible—one that even became a festival God’s people were to celebrate, highlighted several times throughout the year. And though that was in the Old Testament, we find this term in the New Testament as well.

It actually fits well with the series we’ve been looking at all summer long: living a fruitful life, where Jesus reminded us that because of our connection with him through faith, we will produce much fruit. So we’ve been studying what’s called the fruits of the Spirit—you see them displayed on our banners here, and we even added a few others that Scripture mentions. But now that we’re in a new season—this fall season—when our focus isn’t on growing but on harvesting, we’re going to look at what it means to harvest these fruits in our life. That brings us to today’s message: **Living the First Fruits Harvest.**

I’ll share several Bible passages throughout this message, with an emphasis on first fruits. I’ll start with this one from Proverbs: “Honor the Lord with your wealth, with the firstfruits of all your crops.”

It’s probably important that we begin by understanding what the “first fruits” teaching actually means. When the Lord brought the people of Israel out of 400 years of slavery in Egypt and was leading them to the Promised Land, he gave them instructions for their life together, and one of the festivals he wanted them to celebrate was the Festival of First Fruits.

Here’s what they were to do: in spring, as the first fruits of the harvest began coming in, they were to identify the best of the harvest, gather it together, and then, a couple of days later, bring it to the temple of the Lord. There, the priest would hold it up and wave it as a sign that this was a blessing from God—that God had given them this harvest, and they were thankful for it.

It’s also interesting to note when this festival began: on the day of Passover, when they were celebrating the fact that God had brought them out of slavery in Egypt—and picturing how he would also bring them out of slavery to sin and death through the Savior he would send into the world.

On that first day of Passover, they were to go out to the fields. They would mark the field set for harvest by cutting some of the best stalks and binding them together in bundles of three—a sign that three days later they would bring this offering to the Lord.

Now, we don’t worship in that way today, but I think there are important lessons here for us. What was the meaning behind this festival that made it worth celebrating for thousands of years? It was a celebration of dedication. The Lord was reminding them to give thanks for all the blessings they had, recognizing they came from him. Those blessings were theirs, but they were meant to be used properly—for their own good and for his glory. So it was a time of thanksgiving, but also a time of “thanks-living.”

Now, while it’s true we don’t follow that particular festival, custom, or tradition today, there’s something we can learn and live out from it. Just as they had to, we should recognize all the blessings God has given us, and then, with hearts full of thankfulness, worship him and live according to his ways.

You might say, “Well, that sounds like Thanksgiving.” In a way, it is—except there’s a difference. I think the holiday of Thanksgiving has become watered down, forgetful of its purpose. I’m not sure a lot of people are actually giving thanks to God on Thanksgiving. I think they see it as a day off, a gathering of family and friends, some good food, watching football—and enjoying the day if their team wins. But that Old Testament festival was much more than a single Thanksgiving day. It was meant to be lived out every day, for everything, because they would enjoy that harvest all year long. At other times of the year they were told to do the very same thing. That’s a lesson for us: every day, for everything we have, give thanks to God and live his way.

Now that we understand what first fruits meant, let’s identify the first fruits God has placed in our lives. For the Israelites, God focused their attention on their crops—the physical blessings and possessions they had. But it’s interesting to see how, in the New Testament, the focus of “first fruits” shifts to people—and in particular, to the most important person in our lives, Christ Jesus.

I mentioned that the Festival of First Fruits was celebrated on the first day of Passover. Remember Passover: they were to sacrifice a lamb, and originally in Egypt they put the blood over the doorposts as a sign that the angel of death would pass over them because they were God’s people—and so they were spared, given life, and set free from slavery. Passover simply pointed ahead to Jesus, the Lamb of God, who would be sacrificed to take away all our sins.

It’s interesting to trace the steps of that festival. The first thing they did on Passover day was send men out to the fields to identify the sheaves of wheat to be cut down. Perhaps that was around the same time the high priest was interrogating Jesus and calling for him to be “cut down.” When the harvesters found those sheaves, they bound them together and carried them off—perhaps around the same time Roman soldiers were binding Jesus to send him to the cross. But it wasn’t until three days later that the sheaf was actually brought to the temple, where the priest held it up in thanksgiving to God. Well, three days later, Jesus held his arms up in victory as he rose from the dead and came out of the tomb.

Am I stretching things, trying to find connections to Jesus in this festival? I don’t think so—because an important rabbi who later went by the name of the Apostle Paul wrote: “Christ has indeed been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep.” Paul understood exactly what that festival of first fruits was about. It was about acknowledging and celebrating Christ as the first fruits of the resurrection. “First fruits” implies there would be a second, a third, a fourth—and so on. He was talking about us.

Why will we be raised from the dead? Why will God give us life? Because of another offering Jesus made. A little earlier, before Paul spoke of Christ as the first fruits of the resurrection, he wrote this: “What I received I passed on to you as of first importance: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, and that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures.” Jesus died, paying the penalty for our sins. He carried our guilt to the cross and suffered the wrath of God in our place. And in our place in the tomb, he also came alive again. The first fruits of those raised to eternal life—that’s you and me.

Scripture also identifies “first fruits” as believers—those who have come to faith in Jesus as their Savior. The Apostle Paul, in his second letter to the Thessalonian Christians, writes: “But we ought always to thank God for you, brothers and sisters loved by the Lord, because God chose you as firstfruits to be saved through the sanctifying work of the Spirit and through belief in the truth.” God saw us as the first fruits among believers. Those harvesters went out, examined the sheaves of wheat, picked out the best ones, and gave those to the Lord.

What did the Lord see in us that made him choose us? Was it that we were so good—better than others? No—because what he’d actually see is the rottenness and death of our sin. But what he saw instead was Christ, who gave his life as our substitute. He saw the goodness of Jesus in us, the mark the Holy Spirit placed on us—that through faith we have the righteousness of Jesus, and are chosen by God to be first fruits.

James describes it this way: “He chose to give us birth through the word of truth, that we might be a kind of firstfruits of all he created.” We are first fruits—meaning there would also be a second, third, and fourth harvest. I’m talking about your children. I’m talking about the next generation. If we, the believers, are the first fruits, then the “second fruits” are those sitting right next to you—the ones who live with you, the ones who live around you in this community. They’re the ones we’re called to reach out to with the word of truth, so that they too may have salvation through God in Jesus Christ.

Not only have we been redeemed to be God’s people—God also promises that because of Christ’s resurrection, we too will rise. Jesus said, “I am the resurrection and the life. The one who believes in me will live, even though they die.” And Paul explains the order of this: “Each in turn: Christ, the firstfruits; then, when he comes, those who belong to him.” We will be the second, third, fourth—and so on—fruits raised to eternal life.

A harvest of first fruits is sitting right here.

In the Old Testament, “first fruits” referred to the possessions and blessings the people had received from God, which they were to dedicate back to him—and that’s true for us in the New Testament too. God wants us to take our possessions and use them in godly ways, recognizing that the first fruits are what we give back to the Lord. We give to the Lord through our offering of money, time, and talents in service to him, and that is a fruit of faith. Because when we give what we think should be ours, we’re acknowledging that it was really God who loaned it to us in the first place—and by giving it to God, we lose nothing, because we trust that just as he gave us the first fruits, he will give us the second, third, and fourth. He will give us everything we need.

So you’ve heard how they celebrated first fruits in Old Testament times. How do we celebrate them? By recognizing the first and best fruit from God: Jesus. We recognize and celebrate his death and resurrection as the first fruits of our salvation.

Another time the people celebrated the harvest was on the Day of Pentecost, fifty days after Passover. In the New Testament, we’re told that on that day God sent the Holy Spirit and brought about a great harvest of souls, as thousands heard the gospel and believed. We, too, recognize the work of the Holy Spirit in our own lives and in the lives of others, giving them faith and trust in Jesus for eternal life.

So we celebrate Jesus’ death and resurrection, and the blessings of faith, forgiveness, and peace—not just on the days we call Good Friday, Easter, and Pentecost, but all the time. Every day we have the blessing of forgiveness, peace with God, and the assurance of eternal life. That’s why we are the first fruits, and that’s why we celebrate first fruits in our life.

So let’s talk for a few minutes about how we live as the first fruits harvest.

Whatever crop they gathered, they brought it and dedicated it to the Lord. Living as a “first fruits harvest” means we too are to live lives that are dedicated, consecrated, and set apart for the Lord. This is how the Lord instructed his Old Testament people: “Celebrate the Festival of Harvest with the firstfruits of the crops you sow in your field.” So the work they did was to be done with a sense of dedication in serving the Lord. He continued: “Celebrate the Festival of Ingathering at the end of the year, when you gather in your crops from the field.” So it’s not just one particular day, like a Labor Day, when we thank God for the blessing of work and its fruits—every day of the year we’re to dedicate our lives to serving God.

In the book of Revelation, a picture is used of the harvest of souls. Listen to what it says: “These are those who did not defile themselves with women, for they remained virgins. They follow the Lamb wherever he goes. They were purchased from among mankind and offered as firstfruits to God and the Lamb.” This tells us how we’re to live our lives: following the commands of God, understanding his will that is best for us, and following him—that’s how we dedicate our lives as first-fruit souls of the harvest.

God then gets more specific about how he wants us to worship him as first fruits. Look what he says: “Bring the best of the firstfruits of your soil to the house of the LORD your God.” This is where he wants us to worship him. He even gave them the very words to speak, in Deuteronomy: “I now bring the firstfruits of the soil that you, LORD, have given me.” And he instructs: “Place the basket before the LORD your God and bow down before it.” When we come here, it’s to bow down before the Lord and worship him as our Creator, Savior, and Sanctifier. That’s why King Solomon said, “Honor the LORD with your wealth, with the firstfruits of all your crops.” In the same way, we’re to continue giving first fruits to the Lord.

In the Old Testament book of Leviticus, he said: “Every grain offering you bring to the LORD must be made without yeast, for you are not to burn any yeast or honey in a food offering presented to the LORD… You may bring them to the LORD as an offering of the firstfruits.” In the Old Testament, yeast, or leaven, was a symbol of sinfulness. Just as yeast works its way through an entire batch of dough, sin works its way through all of our life. They were to bring their offerings without a sense of sinfulness—greed, showiness, or pride—but rather as a recognition of blessing from God.

He continues: “Season all your grain offerings with salt. Do not leave the salt of the covenant of your God out of your grain offerings; add salt to all your offerings.” I’m sure the people who count our offerings here wouldn’t appreciate it if we dumped salt all over our cash and checks! This is “the salt of the covenant of God.” We use salt to bring out the flavor in our food, and in the same way, God wants us to bring out the flavor of his grace in all our blessings. So whatever we give to the Lord, add that salt of his grace—because that’s what this gift really is: a reflection of his grace.

When the Israelites heard this, here’s what they did: “As soon as the order went out, the Israelites generously gave the firstfruits of their grain, new wine, olive oil and honey, and all that the fields produced. They brought a great amount, a tithe of everything.” They recognized that everything they had was a gift from God, and they brought a thankful heart to the Lord.

So the Apostle Paul, in the New Testament, gives this instruction about the collection for the Lord’s people: “Now about the collection for the Lord’s people: Do what I told the Galatian churches to do. On the first day of every week, each one of you should set aside a sum of money in keeping with your income, saving it up, so that when I come no collections will have to be made.” God wants us, with thankful and thoughtful purpose, to give our offerings to him, reflecting our thankfulness for his grace.

Finally, our first-fruits offering also comes in the form of serving God. Our time and abilities are also gifts from God, which we give back to him in service. The prophet Ezekiel said, “The best of all the firstfruits and of all your special gifts will belong to the priests.” That is, everything we have is to be given to the Lord. And Paul in Romans says, “Not only so, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for our adoption to sonship, the redemption of our bodies.” While we’re here on this earth, let’s take the first fruits of all the blessings we have and dedicate them to serving the Lord.

So, “first fruits”—what was that really about? It was never about how much you give, but why you give to the Lord. It’s your worship—showing thankfulness to him for all he has done for you.

Reading a bit of history this week, I came across the story of King Frederick William III of Prussia, back in the early 1800s. His nation had been going through a devastating period of war—they were nearly finished. He could simply have surrendered to the enemy. Instead, he looked at all the resources his people had. So he called upon the women—because the men were all off fighting—to bring him their gold and silver, which could be melted down to produce weaponry, and they won the war. In exchange for that gold and silver, he gave them medals of iron and bronze.

Think about that: giving up the value of gold and silver, and receiving iron and bronze in return. But the women joyfully did it, because they knew their offering had a purpose. Now, we might see iron as less valuable than gold or silver, but it serves a wonderful purpose. Iron, of course, is very strong. But did you know you also have iron in your body? It’s needed in your blood, to carry oxygen from your lungs into your bloodstream, and out to your muscles to strengthen them, and to your brain to fuel your memory.

In the same way, God gives us the “oxygen” of his grace in return for the gifts we give him—because those gifts allow his grace to reach us, and to reach even more people.

So let us reflect the fact that we are first fruits in God’s harvest, and let us give him our very best. Amen.
