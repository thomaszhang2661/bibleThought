## Sermon: Living with Self-Control

**Opening Prayer**

Heavenly Father, as we have come into your house today, it is your Spirit that moved us to come here — not the free air conditioning. We come here with hearts filled with joy, maybe filled with some burdens too. But we come here freely to be lifted up by your grace. Accept our praise in Jesus’ name.

**Scripture Reading: James, Chapter 4**

James, one of the authors of the New Testament who speaks to us about self-control, addresses his audience by pointing out the different troubles they were having living in society together — how they were arguing with each other, and even stealing from each other. He says it’s because they lack self-control over their desires. He then points us to where self-control starts: in our heart — not by our own will and power, but by the grace that God gives us.

> What causes fights and quarrels among you? Don’t they come from your desires that battle within you? You desire but do not have, so you kill. You covet but you cannot get what you want, so you quarrel and fight. You do not have because you do not ask God. When you ask, you do not receive, because you ask with wrong motives, that you may spend what you get on your pleasures. You adulterous people, don’t you know that friendship with the world means enmity against God? Therefore, anyone who chooses to be a friend of the world becomes an enemy of God. Or do you think Scripture says without reason that he jealously longs for the Spirit he has caused to dwell in us? But he gives us more grace. That is why Scripture says: “God opposes the proud but shows favor to the humble.” Submit yourselves, then, to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Come near to God and he will come near to you. Wash your hands, you sinners, and purify your hearts, you double-minded. Grieve, mourn, and wail. Change your laughter to mourning and your joy to gloom. Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will lift you up.

**Jesus’ Teaching (Matthew, Chapter 4)**

Jesus also teaches us about self-control. If it is God’s Spirit who works this in us, where do we get the Spirit and the direction and guidance we need?

Jesus was tempted to sin. He, of course, never gave in — thankfully, because by his righteous life, you and I are considered righteous by God through faith in him. But Jesus also demonstrates for us where we find the strength for self-control: from the Spirit, who comes to us in God’s Word. This was all demonstrated for us as Jesus himself was tempted by the devil, as we hear in the account in Matthew chapter four:

> Then Jesus was led by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil. After fasting forty days and forty nights, he was hungry. The tempter came to him and said, “If you are the Son of God, tell these stones to become bread.” Jesus answered, “It is written: ‘Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God.’”
> 
> Then the devil took him to the holy city and had him stand on the highest point of the temple. “If you are the Son of God,” he said, “throw yourself down. For it is written: ‘He will command his angels concerning you, and they will lift you up in their hands, so that you will not strike your foot against a stone.’” Jesus answered him, “It is also written: ‘Do not put the Lord your God to the test.’”
> 
> Again, the devil took him to a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their splendor. “All this I will give you,” he said, “if you will bow down and worship me.” Jesus said to him, “Away from me, Satan! For it is written: ‘Worship the Lord your God and serve him only.’” Then the devil left him, and angels came and attended him.

Jesus exercised self-control over temptation for our sake — more than just to set an example for us, but to give us what we all need: the righteousness of God.

**Profession of Faith: “I Will Walk with the Lord”**

> I will walk with the Lord, for it is he who has given me life. Heavenly Father, you created my inmost being; you knit me together in my mother’s womb. I praise you because I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Your works are wonderful; I know that full well.
> 
> I will walk with the Lord, for it is he who has saved me. Loving Savior, I know that it was not with perishable things such as silver or gold that you redeemed me from the empty way of life handed down to me from my ancestors, but with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or defect. He was chosen before the creation of the world but was revealed in these last times for me. Through him I believe in God, who raised him from the dead and glorified him, and so my faith and hope are in God.
> 
> I will walk with the Lord, for it is he who is sanctifying me. Holy Spirit, when the set time had fully come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, to redeem those under the law, that I might receive adoption into his family, because I am his child. God sent the Spirit of his Son into my heart, the Spirit who calls out, “Abba, Father.” I surrender all.

### Sermon Body

What do you mean? Well, it sounds like Pastor didn’t prepare anything today — maybe all of a sudden he woke up and realized it’s Sunday morning, or maybe he doesn’t really know what he should be preaching about today. Well, I can assure you neither of those is true. But “TBD” — to be determined — is a good way, maybe, to describe or start off the topic we’re talking about today: **self-control**. It’s next on that list of fruits of the Spirit that the apostle talked about.

Now, when we think of self-control, maybe we think of those situations where we weren’t displaying it. Maybe we got upset at somebody when we were driving and they cut us off, and so we said something. Or maybe we didn’t display self-control when we saw all those good snacks available and just had to get our hands on them and enjoy them. Or maybe we recognize a lack of self-control when it comes to using our phone — any time we have a little bit of free time or don’t know what to do, we just take out that phone and work away on that screen.

Well, while it may be good for us to practice self-control in those situations, that’s not really what we’re talking about today. We’re talking about the self-control we need to exercise in our spiritual life, regarding our morals and our beliefs. It is the next — and indeed the last — item on the list of the fruits of the Spirit that Paul gave us in Galatians chapter five. But even though it’s last on the list, that doesn’t mean it’s the least important. In fact, as we go through this, you’ll see how important this fruit is, and how it can actually help us with all the other fruits. Even the apostles Peter and James teach us about self-control.

And just so you know, there are other fruits of the Spirit Scripture refers to — we’re not done with this series yet; it’s still summer for a few more weeks. But today, let’s talk about living a life with self-control.

**TBD — that is, we need to understand what self-control is, where it comes from, how we’re going to display it, and why.**

#### 1. Where Self-Control Comes From: The Garden of the Heart

Like with any of the fruits we’ve been talking about, we want to recognize where self-control comes from and where it is going to be grown inside of us. So let’s talk about a garden — just as we would have a garden for growing other fruits or vegetables, there is a garden in which we grow self-control.

Let’s start by understanding one word: self-control. The dictionary defines it this way: *the ability to control oneself, in particular one’s emotions and desires, or the expression of them in one’s behavior, especially in difficult, surprising, or challenging situations* — like when something comes up and you just make some kind of immediate response without thinking about it or planning ahead. It’s just a response we make.

Why is it we make those responses? Well, the New Testament reminds us that it indeed is something that we do, and science tells us it happens in our brain, because that’s where our decision process comes from — whether we think it through or it’s automatic. Scientists have done studies of the brain where they can see the electrical activity happening between our neurons, our brain cells. Even when we’re making decisions, they can see certain areas of the brain light up because of the actions or things we’re thinking about. They have also done experiments where they introduce additional electrical activity into our brains and observe where the brain starts to function — but that’s mostly in regard to muscle activity or basic physical needs. Not really when it comes to moral or belief-related decisions.

Now, the Scriptures tell us that **the sinful mind is hostile to God**. So while it’s true that our brain helps us make decisions and produce responses, because it’s also sinful, it will do things that are against God. This tells us to look a little deeper — and Scripture does go deeper, telling us where self-control actually grows and starts: **in our heart**.

Jesus explains it this way:

> But the things that come out of a person’s mouth come from the heart, and these defile them. For out of the heart come evil thoughts — murder, adultery, sexual immorality, theft, false testimony, and slander.

So self-control really does come from the heart — but it’s not all bad news. The apostle Paul tells us that as God’s people, we are also **eager to do what is good**. So how did that come about? How did that desire to do what’s good and right develop? Well, that’s also in the heart.

How did that change happen? Paul describes it for us in his letter to Titus:

> For the grace of God has appeared that offers salvation to all people. It teaches us to say no to ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in this present age, while we wait for the blessed hope — the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ, who gave himself for us to redeem us from all wickedness and to purify for himself a people that are his very own, eager to do what is good.

That’s where the change happened. That sinful mind, that sinful heart that desires wicked things, has been purified by God. God sent Jesus as the sign of his grace to us. Jesus came into the world and lived under all the commands and laws of God that you and I are under — laws we cannot obey because of our sinful nature. Jesus lived them perfectly, as we saw an example of earlier in the Scripture reading. He did it for us, to give us the very thing we need to be able to stand in the presence of a holy God in heaven: **righteousness** — because Jesus was righteous for us. You and I have that righteousness of Jesus given to us through our faith in him.

Then Jesus took upon himself our guilt for all of our sin — the sins of the whole world — and suffered the punishment of God for them when he suffered on the cross. Now you and I are forgiven because the penalty has been paid, and the proof of that is Jesus’ resurrection from the grave. God raised him from the dead to signal to us that the curse of death has been removed, and, like Jesus, you and I will live forever. He redeemed us to be his very own, and that’s how our heart has been changed, so that we are eager to do what is good.

Yet there is still a battle going on in us between our sinful nature and the Spirit of God. So we have to look at how we can grow this self-control in the garden of our heart and mind.

#### 2. How to Grow Self-Control: Ask God, Train Like an Athlete

Since this is a fruit that **the Spirit** grows, we have to ask the Spirit for it. And here’s the good news: I’m sure there are a lot of things on your prayer list — health, safety, a comfortable life, good finances, job security, good relationships, all of those things. Most of the time, I think God gives us those things, but sometimes maybe he withholds some of them. But you know what? **He wants us to have these fruits.** So, since he wants us to have them, when we ask for them, God is certainly going to answer that prayer and give us his Spirit.

So the first step in growing self-control is: **ask God for it**.

Paul follows up this list of the fruits of the Spirit by saying, “since we live by the Spirit, let’s keep in step with the Spirit” — meaning, do what he needs you to do. Put another way: let’s **train ourselves** in self-control, knowing what God wants us to do, knowing his will, and knowing that he guides us and strengthens us to do it — and let’s keep training that way.

I use the word “train” because Paul talks about an athlete as an illustration. He talks about athletes who train themselves for their particular sport or skill. We see it today in what athletes do — those who are really serious about their sport devote their whole life to it: not just practicing the particular skill, but their eating, sleeping, and exercise — their whole life is devoted to making themselves winners, to being successful.

Paul writes about this, in regard to self-control, in his letter to the Corinthians, chapter nine:

> Do you not know that in a race all the runners run, but only one gets the prize? Run in such a way as to get the prize. Everyone who competes in the games goes into strict training. They do it to get a crown that will not last, but we do it to get a crown that will last forever. Therefore I do not run like someone running aimlessly; I do not fight like a boxer beating the air. No, I strike a blow to my body and make it my slave, so that after I have preached to others, I myself will not be disqualified for the prize.

He’s talking about self-control — reminding us to train with the Spirit in developing self-control so that we can be successful against temptation. And the reason why — it’s not so that we can brag about something or gain some award or accomplishment in this life. No, he says we do it for an **eternal prize**. We’re running the race of faith in God, and so we get this encouragement: run, imitating Christ.

Think of how Christ demonstrated self-control for us. What did he do when he was tempted? Those had to be pretty serious, powerful temptations. He simply referred to the Word of God — that’s where he found his guidance and his strength. And so he’s teaching us to do that very same thing.

The apostle Paul reminds us:

> God made him, who had no sin, to be sin for us, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.

That’s the race we’re running. He also reminds us:

> Jesus died for all, so that those who live should no longer live for themselves, but for him who died for them and was raised again.

We live a life of self-control, not just so that we can be successful in obeying God, but so that we thank and praise God for making us his redeemed people. We live by grace — not by our own efforts, by our own accomplishments — but all by God’s blessing. Knowing he has made us righteous in Christ, he has also empowered us to say no to sin.

Some of us who lived through the 80s can remember — for those of you who weren’t alive then, I’m talking about the 1980s — First Lady Nancy Reagan came up with an anti-drug program, and she entitled it “**Just Say No**.” That’s all you needed to do to resist the temptation for drugs. Well, I don’t know where she got it from, but that’s what Paul says the righteousness of God does for us: it teaches us to say no, to resist the devil, and he has to leave you alone because of the righteousness God has given us. **Say no to sin.**

So this is your training program: **read the Scriptures**. Jesus relied on them, and so we need to rely on them, learn them, learn what Jesus said and what he did for you, and how he shows you to live now. **Follow his will.** That’s how you grow the fruit of self-control.

#### 3. Why Grow Self-Control: The Cost of Its Absence, and the Benefits of Having It

So why do we want to develop self-control? TBD is not only “to be determined,” but also **to be desired**, and **to be developed**. Here’s why we want to develop it — here are the goals for us to set. I’m going to give you a list of things: some negatives — what we will fail at if we don’t develop self-control — and some positives — what we will gain when we have it.

**Negative #1: Without self-control, we make ourselves victims to Satan**

Peter describes it this way:

> Be alert and of sober mind. Your enemy the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, looking for someone to devour.

Now, every once in a while here in Northern California we’ll hear of a mountain lion coming down into city streets, but for the most part we don’t have roaring lions walking around on our streets. However, the devil does prowl around looking to devour us. He may be in that big screen in your living room, or a smaller one — a laptop, or one you have in your pocket that you pull out and use often. The devil likes to use those things to get at us — whether we’re looking at something or reading something we shouldn’t, or using it to say something about somebody else that we shouldn’t. And we could go on and on. It could be what’s said in the school lunchroom, or what’s said at the workplace. There are all sorts of roaring lions out there looking to devour us. Without self-control, without being sober-minded and alert, we may not recognize those traps, and we fall victim, and fall deep.

That’s why Paul tells us: **take your stand against the devil’s schemes.** They are schemes, they’re tricks — he is so good at it, and, you know, we fall for it. Make your stand against it. Be alert and sober-minded, so that you may pray. We need God’s help to stay faithful, because —

**Negative #2: Without self-control, we fall into sin**

We know we sin, and we do it regularly throughout the day. But there is a danger in sin: just because it happens often, and just because we know we’re forgiven, does not take away the seriousness of sin. James reminds us:

> Each person is tempted when they are dragged away by their own evil desire and enticed. Then, after desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin, and sin, when it is full-grown, gives birth to death.

A lack of self-control will lead to sin, and sin is deadly — so deadly that if we fall deep enough that those desires become habits, and that habit becomes character, and character becomes unfaithfulness, we are destroying our souls. Paul warned about this:

> As I have often told you before, and now tell you again even with tears, many live as enemies of the cross of Christ. Their destiny is destruction, their god is their stomach, and their glory is in their shame. Their mind is set on earthly things.

King Solomon put it a bit more graphically:

> Like a city whose walls are broken through is a person who lacks self-control.

In other words, if we don’t have our defenses up to keep the enemy and his schemes away, by lack of self-control, we will fall.

**Now, the positives — the reasons we should have self-control:**

**Positive #1: Self-control gives us stability and security in times of temptation**, because the temptations are going to come — and they will not stop until we take our last breath.

Peter, having just given us that list of the fruits of the Spirit, goes on to say:

> For if you possess these qualities in increasing measure, they will keep you from being ineffective and unproductive in your knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ… Therefore, my brothers and sisters, make every effort to confirm your calling and election. For if you do these things, you will never stumble, and you will receive a rich welcome into the eternal kingdom of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.

Having self-control to say no to the ways of the devil will keep you on that straight and narrow path to eternal life.

**Positive #2: Self-control also gives us focus as we run our race.**

Could you imagine somebody running a race, being distracted by all sorts of other things, not watching where they’re going, not caring where the finish line is? The writer of the letter to the Hebrews talks about this:

> Since we are surrounded by such a great cloud of witnesses, let us throw off everything that hinders and the sin that so easily entangles, and let us run with perseverance the race marked out for us, fixing our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith.

Self-control — even while we may lose it and fall into sin — will always bring us back to the One who saves us: Jesus. Keep your eyes on him, and on the prize he offers you.

**Positive #3: Finally, when we live with self-control, we live in a way that glorifies God and brings God to other people.**

Again, Paul writes:

> For the grace of God has appeared that offers salvation to all people. It teaches us to say no to ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in this present age, while we wait for the blessed hope — the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ.

That’s the finish line. That’s where we want to run our race, that’s what we want to reach — God’s call for focus and self-control, to his glory.

#### Conclusion

So, self-control — an important fruit to have — will help us develop all these other fruits. And when we live with these fruits, we will experience the blessings of God. Basically, **self-control is giving control of yourself to God, who wants you to be saved.**

**Confession**

Mindful of our need, and mindful of our lack of self-control, let’s confess that need to God and ask for his blessing. Please join me in this confession:

> Holy God, gracious Father, I am sinful by nature and have sinned against you in my thoughts, words, and actions. I have not used self-control to say no to sin, and therefore have fallen prey to the devil. Jesus, my Savior, paid for my sins with his innocent suffering and death. Trusting in him, I pray: God, have mercy on me, a sinner.

**Absolution**

We heard earlier how Jesus kept referring to the Scriptures: “It is written… It is written.” Well, it is written: if we confess our sins to him, he is faithful and just, and will forgive us our sins, because the blood of Jesus Christ washes us clean from all sin.

**You are forgiven.**

May God give you the Spirit, so you can live a life of self-control, and all the things he blesses us with. Amen.
