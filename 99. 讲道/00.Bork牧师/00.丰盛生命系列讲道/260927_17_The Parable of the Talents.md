**Serving One Another — The Parable of the Talents**

That hymn certainly reminded us of the encouragement we receive from God to serve one another. That same encouragement comes to us from the Apostle Paul as well. You may have heard the phrase, “It starts at home.” Whatever the topic may be, well, the Apostle Paul reminds us that our service to the Lord doesn’t just start at home — it starts even more personally, in our hearts. And our first Scripture reading today reminds us to serve others with a humble heart.

**First Reading: Romans 12:3–13**

For by the grace given me I say to every one of you: Do not think of yourself more highly than you ought, but rather think of yourself with sober judgment, in accordance with the faith God has distributed to each of you. For just as each of us has one body with many members, and these members do not all have the same function, so in Christ we, though many, form one body, and each member belongs to all the others. We have different gifts, according to the grace given to each of us. If your gift is prophesying, then prophesy in accordance with your faith; if it is serving, then serve; if it is teaching, then teach; if it is to encourage, then give encouragement; if it is giving, then give generously; if it is to lead, do it diligently; if it is to show mercy, do it cheerfully. Love must be sincere. Hate what is evil; cling to what is good. Be devoted to one another in love. Honor one another above yourselves. Never be lacking in zeal, but keep your spiritual fervor, serving the Lord. Be joyful in hope, patient in affliction, faithful in prayer. Share with the Lord’s people who are in need. Practice hospitality.

That’s Paul’s encouragement to us. If you listen closely, he’s simply giving us very concrete ways to serve one another — but with a humble heart.

Jesus taught that same thing to His disciples, when two of them one day asked for positions of honor in heaven. Jesus reminded them that true honor is given to those who serve as He does — not seeking to be served, not seeking honor, but serving the needs of others.

**Second Reading: Mark 10:35–45**

Then James and John, the sons of Zebedee, came to him. “Teacher,” they said, “we want you to do for us whatever we ask.” “What do you want me to do for you?” he asked. They replied, “Let one of us sit at your right and the other at your left in your glory.” “You don’t know what you are asking,” Jesus said. “Can you drink the cup I drink, or be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with?” “We can,” they answered. Jesus said to them, “You will drink the cup I drink, and be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with, but to sit at my right or left is not for me to grant. These places belong to those for whom they have been prepared.” When the ten heard about this, they became indignant with James and John. Jesus called them together and said, “You know that those who are regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their high officials exercise authority over them. Not so with you. Instead, whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, and whoever wants to be first must be slave of all. For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.”

These are the rules our Lord has laid down for us. What moves us to serve as Jesus served is knowing what He did for us — serving us with His light, to give us eternal life. Let’s confess that together in Luther’s explanation to the Second Article:

“I believe that Jesus Christ, true God, begotten of the Father from eternity, and also true man, born of the Virgin Mary, is my Lord. He has redeemed me, a lost and condemned creature, purchased and won me from all sins, from death, and from the power of the devil — not with gold or silver, but with His holy, precious blood, and with His innocent suffering and death. All this He has done in order that I may be His own, live under Him in His kingdom, and serve Him in everlasting righteousness, innocence, and blessedness — just as He is risen from the dead, lives, and reigns to all eternity.”

## “For Heaven’s Sake!” — A Lesson in Talents

Sometimes we say “for heaven’s sake” out of frustration or surprise. But if you think about what those words point to, they can actually give some real guidance for our lives.

For the past several weeks we’ve been talking about living a fruitful life, looking at the fruits of the Spirit that God produces in us. And lately, we’ve been asking: once that fruit is harvested, what do we do with it? That’s exactly what Jesus tells us in one of His parables — a story about a harvest of blessings that has been entrusted to us.

This story is sometimes called the Parable of the Talents, but because a “talent” was a unit of money — and a large sum at that — it’s now often translated as the Parable of the Bags of Gold. Listen to Jesus tell the story again.

**Third Reading: Matthew 25:14–30**

“It will be like a man going on a journey, who called his servants and entrusted his wealth to them. To one he gave five bags of gold, to another two bags, and to another one bag, each according to his ability. Then he went on his journey. The man who had received five bags of gold went at once and put his money to work and gained five bags more. So also the one with two bags of gold gained two more. But the man who had received one bag went off, dug a hole in the ground, and hid his master’s money.

After a long time the master of those servants returned and settled accounts with them. The man who had received five bags of gold brought the other five. ‘Master,’ he said, ‘you entrusted me with five bags of gold. See, I have gained five more.’ His master replied, ‘Well done, good and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things. Come and share your master’s happiness!’

The man with two bags of gold also came. ‘Master,’ he said, ‘you entrusted me with two bags of gold; see, I have gained two more.’ His master replied, ‘Well done, good and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things. Come and share your master’s happiness!’

Then the man who had received one bag of gold came. ‘Master,’ he said, ‘I knew that you are a hard man, harvesting where you have not sown and gathering where you have not scattered seed. So I was afraid, and went out and hid your gold in the ground. See, here is what belongs to you.’ His master replied, ‘You wicked, lazy servant! So you knew that I harvest where I have not sown and gather where I have not scattered seed? Well then, you should have put my money on deposit with the bankers, so that when I returned I would have received it back with interest. Take the bag of gold from him and give it to the one who has ten bags. For whoever has will be given more, and they will have an abundance. Whoever does not have, even what they have will be taken from them. And throw that worthless servant outside, into the darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.’”

---

Do you enjoy a good story? People like stories with a bit of mystery, a bit of action — maybe comedy, maybe romance. But how about a story that’s about _you_ — and God is the one telling it, so there must be some real truth in it? This story we just heard from Jesus is exactly that kind of story. It’s a story God is telling us about the kingdom of heaven.

Jesus says, “The kingdom of heaven will be like…” — He was constantly teaching parables about the kingdom. In fact, He had just finished one telling His disciples to be ready, because they don’t know the day or the hour He’ll return. So He tells us about a man — a master with a great estate, a large fortune — who is going away on a journey. That’s Jesus, who was about to leave His disciples and return to heaven. But before He left, He took all the wealth of His kingdom and entrusted it to His servants, to put it to work.

Jesus loved talking about the kingdom of heaven; many of His parables are about it. Sometimes we hear a story and think, “Well, that’s just a story — it’s not real.” No — these are real stories. There is real truth behind them.

As I considered the different parables Jesus told, I noticed four key things He liked to talk about.

**First, He loved to talk about God Himself.**

Think of the parable of the shepherd with a hundred sheep, one of which wandered off. What did the shepherd do? He left the ninety-nine and went searching for that one sheep, and when he found it, he laid it on his shoulders and brought it home rejoicing. That story tells us about God’s faithful, diligent searching and calling for sinners to return to Him.

In that same chapter, Luke records another story where God — or Jesus — is the central figure: what we call the Parable of the Prodigal Son, though it might better be called the Parable of the Loving Father. A father divided his inheritance between his two sons. One of them went off and squandered his wealth in wild, reckless living. When he had nothing left and realized how low he had sunk, he said, “I will go back to my father.” And as he made his way home, Jesus tells us the father had always been watching that road, and when he saw his son coming, he ran to him, welcomed him with open arms. He didn’t scold him — “I told you so” — but instead put a robe on him and threw a great feast, saying, “This son of mine was lost and is found.”

Jesus loved telling of God’s love for people — but even more, He loved explaining _why_ God loves us and how we are welcomed back. Jesus said, “The time has come. The kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news.” Elsewhere He said, “I must proclaim the good news of the kingdom of God to the other towns also, because that is why I was sent.” Jesus loved proclaiming that good news — the grace of God that opens the kingdom of heaven to us; the good news that our sins have been removed, placed on Jesus, and He suffered for them; that our sins have been replaced by Jesus’ righteousness, so we stand righteous before God; and that because of His resurrection, we too will rise from the dead and live forever. That’s the good news Jesus loved weaving into His parables. In fact, those bags of gold given to the servants in today’s parable are an illustration of that very good news.

As I mentioned, this parable is sometimes called the Parable of the Talents because a “talent” was the coin used — a sum equal to about forty years’ wages for a common laborer in New Testament times. I’m not sure what a common worker’s wage is here in the Bay Area — maybe $80,000 a year. Can you imagine being handed a bag worth five or ten years’ wages, and being told by your boss, “Go invest it”? Jesus used that image to emphasize that His grace doesn’t merely supply us with a year’s income — it gives us eternal life.

**Second, Jesus loved to talk about the growth of the kingdom** — how it grows simply through the spreading of the Word. Remember the Parable of the Sower? Seed cast on the ground, and maybe it grows — I say “maybe,” because sometimes the soil doesn’t receive it: it’s hard, or birds snatch the seed away, or weeds grow up and choke the young plant. Jesus said sometimes the cares, concerns, and pleasures of this world choke out our faith. So He loved describing how the kingdom grows simply through the gospel message going out, through grace being proclaimed.

**Third, Jesus loved to talk about the glory of the kingdom of God** — speaking of Himself as the King who will one day say to believers, “Come, you who are blessed by my Father; take your inheritance, the kingdom prepared for you.” That’s what Jesus loved to talk about.

But in today’s parable, He is also speaking about us — about an assignment, a responsibility He has given us, which we call ministry: serving others. Just as the man in the story gave valuable bags of gold to his servants and wanted them put to work, Jesus has likewise given us a responsibility. We call it a _trust_. We have received a noble trust from the Lord — to spread His grace to others so that they may be saved.

And yet, notice the different responses of those who received the bags of gold. Two of them took the assignment seriously, invested the money, and it produced a return. But one did not — we’ll talk about why in a moment.

You and I have been given this same responsibility as servants of the Lord. Those who know and have received His grace have been told to share that grace with others. In the parable, Jesus says they were each given bags of gold “according to their ability” — and you and I have likewise been given abilities. The Apostle Paul says there are different kinds of gifts, but the same Spirit distributes them; different kinds of service, but we all serve the same Lord; different kinds of working, but in all of them it is the same God at work. So you and I have been given a trust — a responsibility, a noble trust — and we are to put it to work. In fact, what is required of us is that we be found faithful in using it.

When the workers returned and reported to their master what they had done, he commended them: “Well done, good and faithful servant.” That’s what God expects — that we be faithful in carrying out this responsibility. The Apostle Paul puts it simply: “Now it is required that those who have been given a trust must prove faithful.”

Each servant had different abilities — one had five, another two, another one. That the last man had only one ability didn’t mean it was less important or useless; he was to do exactly what the others did — put it to work. But he proved unfaithful. His excuse: “I was afraid, because you demanded too much, and I didn’t think I could meet the requirement.” The master simply called him lazy.

Jesus reminds us how important it is to put this trust to work. In the parable He gave just before this one, He said: keep watch, because you do not know the day or the hour. That’s true not only for us, but for everyone. We don’t know when Jesus will return, but He is coming back for all — will they be ready? Jesus has given us His grace, a trust, and an assignment to share that grace with others. Will we prove faithful in doing it?

When those men reported what they had done, they received a reward for their faithfulness. Hearing that, our curiosity is naturally piqued — what reward might God give me for being faithful? But notice: Jesus never dwells on the reward in the story, because that’s not the point. The focus isn’t “what do I get out of this,” but rather, “what do I already have, and how thankful am I for it” — and responding to the Lord in faithfulness to what He has asked of us.

Still, we do get a hint of what that return looks like. Jesus says, “Come and share in your master’s happiness.” God promises that we will experience His joy — not only in heaven, but even here on earth — as we carry out the work He has given us to do. So our service itself carries its own sweetness; it’s guaranteed by God.

As for the man who had one responsibility and simply set it aside, doing nothing with it — it was taken from him, and Jesus said he was thrown outside into the darkness, where there is weeping and gnashing of teeth. In other words: no joy. We don’t take this to mean Jesus is saying he’s condemned to hell (he still believed in his Lord), but rather that he would miss out on the joy of serving God. And Jesus’ point is also this: when you have done everything you were told to do, you should simply say, “We are unworthy servants; we have only done our duty.”

A few minutes ago I told you this story is about you. Do you recognize yourself in it? Can you identify with the faithful servants — thankful for the responsibility given them, thankful for God’s grace, faithfully carrying out their work, and receiving the joy of their master? Do you recognize the blessings God has given you — His grace, your salvation, and the opportunity to serve others?

Or do you find yourself identifying more with the one who responded out of fear, or with a “that’s not my responsibility, I don’t know what to do” kind of attitude — someone with no real concern for what God was asking of him?

Let us respond with faithfulness — with a thankful response to the grace God has given us — and recognize the privilege we’ve been given to serve in His kingdom.

## Let’s Talk About the Work of the Kingdom

First, let’s recognize that this is something God has entrusted to us, and so there is a sense of accountability attached to it. When the master in the story returned, he held his servants accountable — he wanted to know what they had done with what was given them. Let us recognize that what God asks of us is simply to be about our Father’s business. Ministry is something God has designed; He is the one who assigns it to us, and He is the one who will bless it. Whatever we do in His name and with His Word, God will bless it. The Apostle Paul reminds us: “I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ; I will not hide it. I will put it to work, because it is the power of God for salvation” — the gospel that saves people.

That’s the Father’s business entrusted to us. So let’s be about doing the Father’s business. Let’s look for the opportunities placed before us and respond joyfully and faithfully. Let’s not be like the servant who thought, “How little can I get away with,” rather than “What more can I do?” — because the other two servants were given even more responsibility in God’s kingdom as a result of their faithfulness.

We might assume that if God has given us some kind of assignment, it must be something big and spectacular. But that’s not always so — at least not in our human way of thinking. Even the small opportunities that surround us every day — the person sitting next to you in the pew, the ones who live in your house, work in your workplace, or attend your school — these are the opportunities God has given us to share His gospel. The only thing He asks of us is faithfulness in doing it.

Do you hear Jesus calling you? If you do, then make the investment. We talk about investing our money — in a savings account, in interest, in the stock market — hoping for a return. Well, we are promised a return from investing those “bags of gold” — God’s grace — in others. So what we need to do is see the opportunity and seize it, putting it to work.

Here are four areas to consider:

**First, our time.**

Many of us would say, “I don’t have time.” But we all have the same twenty-four hours each day — the question is how we use it. Some of that time is needed for ourselves, to rest and restore our bodies. Some of it goes toward work, family, and other responsibilities — all legitimate, and we should manage that time well and not neglect those duties. But there is also time God wants us to spend on His work, in worship, in building our faith through His Word, and in serving others.

**Second, our talents and abilities.**

It’s fitting that the coin in the parable was called a “talent,” since we now use that word for our abilities. The Apostle Peter wrote about this: “Each of you should use whatever gift you have received to serve others, as faithful stewards of God’s grace in its various forms. If anyone speaks, they should do so as one who speaks the very words of God. If anyone serves, they should do so with the strength God provides, so that in all things God may be praised through Jesus Christ.”

So look for and seize opportunities to use your abilities. Peter mentions speaking — using words. That could mean preaching (not everyone is called to that, but perhaps some young people should consider it — maybe that’s exactly what God has in mind for you), or teaching in Bible classes, Sunday school, or children’s ministry. It could be less formal — simply speaking words of encouragement. We have an encouragement group that reaches out to visitors and newcomers; a “heart-to-heart” group that visits those who are homebound due to illness or age; greeters who welcome people to church; people who act as shepherds, guiding others in their walk of faith; people who love to pray on behalf of others; people who love to write. If you enjoy using words, there are many ways you can serve God with them.

Peter also speaks of serving — that is, working, using your skills and abilities. That might look like leading a group, serving in a leadership role, helping with stewardship or finances, working in youth ministry, or ushering. Later this month we have a Trunk-or-Treat event for our school children — maybe you can help there. Maybe you can serve in our bookstore or library, offer hospitality, help in children’s ministry, or join our “Willing Hands” group — people who say, “I’ll help with that project at your house.” You might use musical gifts in worship, help with our tech or livestream team, or simply help maintain our church property. Maybe you’re artistically inclined and could help decorate the church — we’re looking for people to join our flower committee, putting fresh flowers on the altar each week, and with the holidays approaching, we’ll want to dress up the building a bit — let us know if you’d like to help with that. The point is simply: see the opportunity, and seize it with the abilities you enjoy using.

**Third, our treasure.**

It’s no coincidence that “talent” originally referred to a coin — so we can also serve God with our finances. Scripture reminds us to honor God with our wealth. The Apostle Paul wrote, “On the first day of every week, each one of you should set aside a sum of money in keeping with your income, saving it up, so that when I come no collections will have to be made.” God wants us to use our income to support the work of the church — our congregation, our school, our community outreach, and the missionaries we support here and around the world — putting those bags of gold to work for people.

**Fourth, our talk.**

Finally, it all comes down to our message. God simply wants us to share the gospel. Paul wrote, “Let your conversation be always full of grace, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone.” Peter encouraged the same: “In your hearts revere Christ as Lord. Always be prepared to give an answer to everyone who asks you the reason for the hope that you have — but do it with gentleness and respect.”

In all these ways — our time, our talent, our treasure, and our talk — we have been entrusted with the gospel, and we can invest all four to advance the kingdom of God.

You might wonder, “How do I know what it is I’m supposed to do?” Simply look at your talents, your resources — think about them, talk about them with others, and then try. You’ll find you enjoy it.

Jesus is coming back soon. We don’t know when — but in the meantime, He calls us to watch for Him, to witness of Him to others, and to work for His kingdom until He calls us home.

All for heaven’s sake. Amen.
